## How to read this

**Opened.** `full` = I obtained the text and read the methods, results and limits cited. `abstract` = abstract only, through Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar or Europe PMC; a few long abstracts were read to about 1,900 characters, not always to the end. `extract` = a summarising fetch tool read the page for me; its wording is not a checked quote. `not opened` = I saw only a search summary or another source's description, or Crossref confirmed the paper exists and nothing more.

**Ratings.** strong = several consistent meta-analyses or large trials on this question. moderate = some good evidence with real limits. weak = thin, small, single-group or only correlational. contested = good evidence pointing different ways. no evidence = I looked and found no measurement. A rating applies to what was measured, not to the writing inference.

**Inference.** Every "Would change (inference)" line is my reasoning about a scene or lesson. No cited study tested it unless the line says so.

**Stimulus warning for the whole file.** Almost nothing here comes from a story or a game. Each claim names its source: students, photos of faces, vignettes, lab tasks, consumers. Where a claim does come from fiction, it says so. Moving a finding from a photo or a trust game to a scene is always inference.

# Strand 1. Motivation and persistence in learning

## Q1. Self-determination theory (SDT)

### P1. Supporting the three needs goes with motivation and well-being; competence is the strongest of the three for students; performance links are smaller

- **Claim.** Interpersonal support for autonomy, competence and relatedness goes strongly with reported need satisfaction and well-being, and small to moderately with performance. Among students, competence predicted self-determined motivation most, then autonomy, then relatedness.
- **Citations.**
  - Bureau, Howard, Chong et al (2021). Pathways to Student Motivation. Review of Educational Research 92:46-72. doi:10.3102/00346543211042426. abstract.
  - Slemp, Field, Ryan et al (2024). Interpersonal supports for basic psychological needs. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 127:1012-1037. doi:10.1037/pspi0000459. abstract.
  - Ryan, Duineveld, Di Domenico et al (2022). We know this much is (meta-analytically) true. Psychological Bulletin 148:813-842. doi:10.1037/bul0000385. abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Bureau: 144 studies, over 79,000 students; no coefficients in the abstract. Slemp: N = 443,556 across samples; effects described in words only; some effects decline with sample age and measurement lag. Ryan: 60 meta-analyses reviewed, led by the theory's founder, so not independent. Nearly all same-source self-report and correlational.
- **Replication.** Many meta-analyses agree on the association; none separates cause. Also [L-C21].
- **Rating.** strong for association with self-report. moderate for performance. Not evidence of cause.
- **Would change (inference).** When a reviewer must pick one need for a beginner, competence is the first candidate. It doesn't show that adding autonomy or relationship features would buy anything.

### P2. Experiments show SDT-style support can be taught and can change behaviour, but game elements don't reliably move felt autonomy

- **Claim.** Teachers can be trained to be more autonomy-supportive, with benefits to students. SDT-based health interventions produced small-to-medium changes. Framing a task around intrinsic goals in an autonomy-supportive way raised depth of processing, test performance and persistence. In one game-element trial, specific elements moved competence and relatedness needs, but decision freedom couldn't be moved as intended.
- **Citations.**
  - Reeve, Cheon (2021). Autonomy-supportive teaching. Educational Psychologist 56:54-77. doi:10.1080/00461520.2020.1862657. abstract.
  - Ntoumanis, Ng, Prestwich et al (2020). SDT-informed intervention studies in health. Health Psychology Review 15:214-244. doi:10.1080/17437199.2020.1718529. abstract.
  - Vansteenkiste, Simons, Lens et al (2004). Motivating Learning, Performance, and Persistence. JPSP 87:246-260. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.87.2.246. abstract.
  - Deci, Eghrari, Patrick et al (1994). Facilitating Internalization. Journal of Personality 62:119-142. doi:10.1111/j.1467-6494.1994.tb00797.x. abstract.
  - Sailer, Hense, Mayr et al (2017). How gamification motivates. Computers in Human Behavior 69:371-380. doi:10.1016/j.chb.2016.12.033. abstract.
  - Peng, Lin, Pfeiffer et al (2012). Need Satisfaction Supportive Game Features. Media Psychology 15:175-196. doi:10.1080/15213269.2012.673850. abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Reeve: narrative review by advocates of 51 interventions including 38 randomised trials. Ntoumanis: 73 experimental studies, health, not learning. Vansteenkiste: three field experiments, high school and college, all ps < .001, no effect sizes in the abstract; how persistence was measured not read. Deci: lab; a meaningful rationale, acknowledging feelings and conveying choice promoted internalisation. Sailer: randomised, online simulation, not a game with play over time. Peng: 2 x 2 exergame features; outcomes included motivation for future play, not actual return.
- **Replication.** Theory's own groups mostly. Sailer is independent of the founders.
- **Rating.** moderate for teachers and short tasks. weak for games, where the outcomes are intentions.
- **Would change (inference).** Deci's three factors are a usable review test for any instruction that asks a learner to do something dull: is the reason given, is the feeling acknowledged, is it framed as a choice. Don't assume adding branching or customisation raises felt autonomy (see also [M17], [L-C20]).

### P3. Does need satisfaction predict persistence? School dropout, yes as a correlate. Next-day game play, barely

- **Claim.** In a prospective school study, perceived competence and autonomy fed self-determined motivation, dropout intentions and actual dropout. But in logged game data, need satisfaction went with how people felt, and showed weak or null links with what they next did.
- **Citations.**
  - Ballou, Földes, Vuorre, Hakman, Magnusson, Przybylski (2026). All BANG, little buck: Need-related experiences are weakly linked with behavior in the video game domain. PsyArXiv preprint, doi:10.31234/osf.io/wqr6u_v1 (status from an `extract` of a university page; no journal version seen). Text from https://vuorre.com/bibliography/files/N3UGW4CS/ballou-etal_2026_all-bang,-little-buck-need-related-expe.pdf . full (abstract, confirmatory results, Study 2 results, limits).
  - Vallerand, Fortier, Guay (1997). Self-determination and persistence in a real-life setting. JPSP 72:1161-1176. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.72.5.1161. abstract.
  - Jeno, Danielsen, Raaheim (2018). Students' academic achievement and dropout. Educational Psychology 38:1163-1184. doi:10.1080/01443410.2018.1502412. abstract.
  - Johannes, Vuorre, Przybylski (2021). Video game play is positively correlated with well-being. Royal Society Open Science 8. doi:10.1098/rsos.202049. abstract.
  - Vuorre, Johannes, Magnusson et al (2022). Time spent playing video games is unlikely to impact well-being. Royal Society Open Science 9. doi:10.1098/rsos.220411. abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Ballou Study 1, preregistered: 555 US players, 30 days, about 11k daily observations with multi-platform logs. A point above one's own average in game need satisfaction and the odds of any play in the next 24 hours: β = 0.04 [95% CI -0.03, 0.12]. Felt link to global need satisfaction: β = 0.24 [0.21, 0.27]. Study 1's outcome is any play on any platform, not return to a game. .04 is log-odds; .24 is scale points from the same survey, so they aren't comparable. Study 2, exploratory: 101,602 sessions, 8,969 players of one game. Session length β = 0.009 [-0.003, 0.021]; time to next session β = -0.044 [-0.082, -0.005], which the authors call small in practice. Limits the authors state: within-person, not causal; engaged adult players; short-term. They call it strong evidence against large, general, short-term effects, not against any role. Vallerand: N = 4,537 high school students, prospective, actual dropout. Jeno: n = 754, cross-sectional, dropout intentions. Johannes and Vuorre: log data with surveys; need satisfaction and motivations related to well-being, independent of play time.
- **Replication.** Ballou's two studies agree. Long-horizon retention in games: none found. Search-result summaries point to survey work on continuance intentions; not opened.
- **Rating.** strong that need satisfaction predicts feeling. contested for persistence: moderate as a correlate of school dropout, weak or null for short-term play. no evidence for long-run retention in a game or for learning-game quitting.
- **Would change (inference).** A need-satisfaction score from a playtest is not a retention forecast. Report it with return data, the same way [G-B7] separates engagement and learning. Matches [M4]: no scale I've seen predicts coming back. Ballou's players were already engaged, so this says little about a beginner deciding to quit in hour two.

### P4. The measures: usable for felt experience, disputed in how they're scored

- **Claim.** SDT's continuum of motivation types is disputed. A Rasch analysis found weak evidence and argued against the relative autonomy index; a large meta-analysis largely supported a continuum-like order, with unstable distances and two components not distinct. Game scales were little validated until recently. HCI games papers mostly cite SDT descriptively.
- **Citations.**
  - Chemolli, Gagné (2014). Evidence against the continuum structure. Psychological Assessment 26:575-585. doi:10.1037/a0036212. abstract.
  - Howard, Gagné, Bureau (2017). Testing a continuum structure. Psychological Bulletin 143:1346-1377. doi:10.1037/bul0000125. abstract.
  - Van den Broeck, Ferris, Chang et al (2016). SDT's basic psychological needs at work. Journal of Management 42:1195-1229. doi:10.1177/0149206316632058. abstract.
  - Johnson, Gardner, Perry (2018). Validation of two game experience scales. IJHCS 118:38-46. doi:10.1016/j.ijhcs.2018.05.003. abstract.
  - Ballou, Denisova, Ryan et al (2024). The Basic Needs in Games Scale. IJHCS 188:103289. doi:10.1016/j.ijhcs.2024.103289. abstract.
  - Tyack, Mekler (2020). Self-Determination Theory in HCI Games Research. CHI 1-22. doi:10.1145/3313831.3376723. abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Chemolli: two scales, Rasch. Howard: 486 samples, over 205,000 people, 13 scales. Johnson: n = 571 players; PENS largely supported after minor revision, GEQ only partly. Ballou 2024: five studies, 1,246 people; written because earlier scales missed need frustration and relatedness in single-player play. Tyack: 110 CHI and CHI PLAY papers. Van den Broeck: 99 studies, cautions on measures; no numbers in the abstract.
- **Replication.** Direct disagreement between Chemolli and Howard, with an author in common.
- **Rating.** moderate that the subscales measure something. contested for the single-index score.
- **Would change (inference).** If a project surveys players, use a game-specific scale with the frustration subscales, and report subscales rather than one index.

## Q2. Expectancy, value and self-efficacy; what keeps a beginner going

### P5. Self-efficacy is among the best correlates of staying and of grades in college

- **Claim.** Academic self-efficacy, goals and study skills each go with retention at about .35; self-efficacy is the best correlate of GPA among psychosocial factors.
- **Citations.**
  - Robbins, Lauver, Le et al (2004). Do Psychosocial and Study Skill Factors Predict College Outcomes? Psychological Bulletin 130:261-288. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.130.2.261. abstract.
  - Richardson, Abraham, Bond (2012). Psychological correlates of university students' academic performance. Psychological Bulletin 138:353-387. doi:10.1037/a0026838. abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Robbins: 109 studies. Retention ρ = .340 goals, .359 self-efficacy, .366 academic skills; GPA ρ = .496 self-efficacy, .303 achievement motivation. Richardson: 241 data sets, 50 correlates; performance self-efficacy was the largest. College students, correlational, between people.
- **Replication.** Two large meta-analyses agree.
- **Rating.** strong for the association in college.
- **Would change (inference).** Competence belief is worth watching in a beginner. Claim 6 limits what follows.

### P6. Within a person, success builds self-efficacy far more than self-efficacy builds success

- **Claim.** Past performance predicts later self-efficacy strongly. Self-efficacy predicts later performance weakly once trends are controlled, and can even run negative.
- **Citations.**
  - Sitzmann, Yeo (2013). The Within-Person Self-Efficacy Domain. Personnel Psychology 66:531-568. doi:10.1111/peps.12035. abstract.
  - Vancouver, Thompson, Williams (2001). The changing signs. Journal of Applied Psychology 86:605-620. doi:10.1037/0021-9010.86.4.605. abstract.
  - Talsma, Schüz, Schwarzer et al (2018). I believe, therefore I achieve (and vice versa). Learning and Individual Differences. doi:10.1016/j.lindif.2017.11.015. not opened (confirmed by Crossref; title indicates both directions).
- **Design, sample, effect.** Sitzmann: self-efficacy to performance ρ = .23, falling to .06 with the linear trajectory controlled; past performance to self-efficacy ρ = .40, remaining .30. Vancouver: 56 undergraduates, within-person, negative effect; then 185 undergraduates where the sign depended on goal difficulty. Outcome is performance, not quitting.
- **Replication.** Vancouver's negative sign depends on conditions; Sitzmann's moderators range from -.02 to .33.
- **Rating.** moderate to strong on direction; no evidence on quitting.
- **Would change (inference).** Confidence lines from a character aren't the lever. A real, checked success is. This fits [L-C12] (feedback on task) and [L-C18] (self-rating isn't learning).

### P7. Beginning programmers read normal events as a verdict on themselves

- **Claim.** Beginners report judging themselves negatively at ordinary moments of programming, such as getting a syntax error or spending time planning, and those who do it more have lower self-efficacy. Speed, grades and prior experience are the measures of ability they use. Mental models and self-efficacy both relate to course performance.
- **Citations.**
  - Gorson, O'Rourke (2020). Why do CS1 Students Think They're Bad at Programming? ICER 170-181. doi:10.1145/3372782.3406273. abstract.
  - Lewis, Yasuhara, Anderson (2011). Deciding to major in computer science. ICER 3-10. doi:10.1145/2016911.2016915. abstract.
  - Kinnunen, Simon (2012). My program is ok – am I? Computer Science Education 22:1-28. doi:10.1080/08993408.2012.655091. abstract.
  - Ramalingam, LaBelle, Wiedenbeck (2004). Self-efficacy and mental models in learning to program. ITiCSE 171-175. doi:10.1145/1007996.1008042. abstract.
  - Lishinski, Yadav, Good et al (2016). Learning to Program. ICER 211-220. doi:10.1145/2960310.2960329. abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Gorson: survey of 214 CS1 students at three universities, vignettes of 13 moments, no causal test. Lewis: interviews, 31 students. Kinnunen: four interviews each with 9 majors; some held negative views even after a good experience. Lishinski: women's self-efficacy connected differently to performance; size not in the abstract. All college.
- **Replication.** Qualitative convergence; the interventions proposed are untested in these papers.
- **Rating.** moderate that this self-reading occurs. no evidence that changing it reduces quitting.
- **Would change (inference).** A scene that shows an expert hitting errors and carrying on addresses a measured belief, but whether it helps is unknown. Test it rather than cite this.

### P8. Why beginners leave: time, falling behind, and how frustration develops

- **Claim.** In interviews, dropouts name lack of time and motivation, cumulative causes, and a point of realising they were behind. In a lab session, frustration and boredom went with lower learning, and movement between confusion and frustration went with slightly higher learning.
- **Citations.**
  - Kinnunen, Malmi (2006). Why students drop out CS1 course? ICER 97-108. doi:10.1145/1151588.1151604. abstract.
  - Petersen, Craig, Campbell et al (2016). Revisiting why students drop CS1. Koli Calling 71-80. doi:10.1145/2999541.2999552. abstract.
  - Bosch, D'Mello (2017). The Affective Experience of Novice Computer Programmers. IJAIED 27:181-206. doi:10.1007/s40593-015-0069-5. abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Kinnunen: 18 dropouts, Finland, course drop-out 30-50%. Petersen: 18 students, North America; completers spoke of new techniques for learning, those who dropped of other priorities or being unable to catch up. Bosch: N = 99 novices, 25 + 10 minutes of Python, retrospective affect judgements. Frustration r = -.218, boredom r = -.149 with learning; confusion to frustration r = .103, frustration to confusion r = .105, boredom to engagement r = .282.
- **Replication.** Interview findings agree across two countries. Bosch single study.
- **Rating.** weak for causes. No trial shows what keeps an adult self-learner from quitting. Closest measurement of the early loss: [G-A12].
- **Would change (inference).** The moment to design for is "I've fallen behind", more than a difficulty spike. Don't remove all confusion, but watch for a run of frustration with no way out.

### P9. Having the learner say why it matters raised grades and retention; hearing why it matters wasn't what was tested

- **Claim.** When college students wrote about how course content connected to their own lives, grades and interest rose, especially for lower performers, and they were more likely to enrol in the next course and stay in the major.
- **Citations.**
  - Hulleman, Harackiewicz (2009). Promoting Interest and Performance in High School Science Classes. Science 326:1410-1412. doi:10.1126/science.1177067. abstract (editor's summary).
  - Hulleman, Kosovich, Barron et al (2017). Making connections. Journal of Educational Psychology 109:387-404. doi:10.1037/edu0000146. abstract.
  - Harackiewicz, Canning, Tibbetts et al (2016). Closing achievement gaps with a utility-value intervention. JPSP 111:745-765. doi:10.1037/pspp0000075. abstract.
  - Canning, Harackiewicz, Priniski et al (2018). Improving performance and retention in introductory biology. Journal of Educational Psychology 110:834-849. doi:10.1037/edu0000244. abstract.
  - Perez, Cromley, Kaplan (2014). Identity development, values, and costs in college STEM retention. Journal of Educational Psychology 106:315-329. doi:10.1037/a0034027. abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Harackiewicz: double-blind randomised, N = 1,040, four semesters; effect for first-generation under-represented students .51 grade points, d = 0.55, gap reduced 61%. Canning: N = 577, randomised dose; any dose raised grades, next-course enrolment and retention in the major. Perez: 363 students, correlational; effort and opportunity cost went with intent to leave.
- **Replication.** Replicated, largely within this research community. Pooled intervention numbers: Lazowski in [L-C21]. Not tested in a game or told by a character.
- **Rating.** moderate for college writing tasks. no evidence for a character stating the reason.
- **Would change (inference).** This sharpens "explain why each skill matters" in teaching-design: the active ingredient tested was the learner producing the connection. A character telling them is a different, untested treatment.

### P10. A brief belonging exercise raised first-year completion at scale, only where the setting allowed belonging

- **Claim.** A 30-minute online exercise before college raised full-time first-year completion across 22 institutions, mostly in groups with lower progress rates, and only in settings that afforded them chances to belong.
- **Citations.**
  - Walton, Murphy, Logel et al (2023). Where and with whom does a brief social-belonging intervention promote progress in college? Science 380:499-505. doi:10.1126/science.ade4420. abstract.
  - Walton, Cohen (2011). A Brief Social-Belonging Intervention. Science 331:1447-1451. doi:10.1126/science.1198364. abstract (one line).
  - Murphy, Gopalan, Carter et al (2020). A customized belonging intervention. Science Advances 6. doi:10.1126/sciadv.aba4677. abstract (one line).
  - Broda, Yun, Schneider et al (2018). Reducing Inequality in Academic Success. JREE 11:317-338. doi:10.1080/19345747.2018.1429037. abstract.
  - Krause-Levy, Griswold, Porter et al (2021). Sense of Belonging and Student Outcomes in CS1 and Beyond. ICER 29-41. doi:10.1145/3446871.3469748. abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Walton 2023: randomised, N = 26,911, no effect size in the abstract. Broda: N = 7,686 at one university; the abstract reports a growth-mindset GPA gain of about .40 for Latino/a students and speaks of "complexity" for belonging; the belonging result not read. Krause-Levy: correlational; lower belonging went with lower pass rates and performance, but was not predictive of persistence to the next CS course outside the first sequence.
- **Replication.** Large confirmatory trial, with mixed single-site results around it. All college.
- **Rating.** moderate to strong for US college first-year progress. no evidence for games or self-learners.
- **Would change (inference).** Evidence that a message's effect depends on the setting backing it up. A reassuring line in a hostile or thin context may do nothing.

### P11. Mindset and attribution: a close replication failed, and the subgroup effect is disputed

- **Claim.** The classic praise-for-intelligence result failed a close replication in Chinese children. Two 2023 meta-analyses dispute subgroup benefits. Attributional retraining has a quasi-experimental college result. One programming class reported gains.
- **Citations.**
  - Li, Bates (2019). You can't change your basic ability. JEP: General 148:1640-1655. doi:10.1037/xge0000669. abstract.
  - Mueller, Dweck (1998). Praise for intelligence. JPSP 75:33-52. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.75.1.33. abstract.
  - Tipton, Bryan, Murray et al (2023). Why meta-analyses of growth mindset should follow best practices. Psychological Bulletin 149:229-241. doi:10.1037/bul0000384. abstract.
  - Yeager, Carroll, Buontempo et al (2021). Teacher Mindsets. Psychological Science 33:18-32. doi:10.1177/09567976211028984. abstract.
  - Perry, Stupnisky, Hall et al (2010). Bad Starts and Better Finishes. Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology 29:668-700. doi:10.1521/jscp.2010.29.6.668. abstract.
  - Cutts, Cutts, Draper et al (2010). Manipulating mindset. SIGCSE 431-435. doi:10.1145/1734263.1734409. abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Li: n = 624, ages 9-13, China; close replication found one result at p = .049 and none across 8 motivation and attribution measures (mean p = .48); active-control studies null (ps .189 to .974). Tipton: re-analysis, with authors of the intervention studies, finds a meaningful effect in focal at-risk groups. Yeager: 9,167 students matched to 223 teachers; effect depends on teacher mindset. Perry: quasi-experimental, 457 first-years; low and average performers improved grades. Cutts: one class, no size in the abstract, not a preregistered trial.
- **Replication.** See [L-C22] for Macnamara, Burnette, Sisk, Yeager 2019. Macnamara and Tipton remain in direct dispute. Children and school mostly; nothing in adult self-learners.
- **Rating.** contested for subgroups; weak for any general effect. weak for attributional retraining as a general tool (one design, one research group seen).
- **Would change (inference).** Keep "no mindset lesson". The reason to avoid "you're a natural" stands on [L-C12], not on Mueller and Dweck.

### P12. Other findings a reviewer should not lean on

- **Claim.** Well-known effects that large replications have weakened or removed.
- **Citations and effects.**
  - **Stereotype threat on test scores.** Flore, Mulder, Wicherts (2018). Registered report, Comprehensive Results in Social Psychology 3:140-174. doi:10.1080/23743603.2018.1559647. abstract. N = 2,064, Dutch, age 13-14: no overall or moderated effect. Zigerell (2017). JAP 102:1159-1168. doi:10.1037/apl0000188. abstract. Bias adjustments from no change to near zero, with moderators possible. Shewach, Sackett, Quint (2019). JAP 104:1514-1534. doi:10.1037/apl0000420. abstract. Operational-like settings: d "ranging from .00 to -.14" (reading of a clipped abstract), publication bias found. Stoet, Geary (2012). Review of General Psychology 16:93-102. doi:10.1037/a0026617. abstract. 30% of unconfounded experiments replicated. Alston, Darity, Eckel et al (2022). JEBO 194:408-424. doi:10.1016/j.jebo.2021.12.005. extract. No effect at a historically Black university, one site.
  - **Ego depletion.** Hagger, Chatzisarantis, Alberts et al (2016). PPS 11:546-573. doi:10.1177/1745691616652873. abstract. k = 23, N = 2,141, d = 0.04 [-0.07, 0.15]. Vohs, Schmeichel, Lohmann et al (2021). Psychological Science 32:1566-1581. doi:10.1177/0956797621989733. abstract. k = 36, N = 3,531, d = 0.06, ns. Dang, Barker, Baumert et al (2020). SPPS 12:14-24. doi:10.1177/1948550619887702. abstract. N = 1,775, d = 0.10, significant.
  - **Physical warmth priming social warmth.** Lynott, Corker, Wortman et al (2014). Social Psychology 45:216-222. doi:10.1027/1864-9335/a000187. abstract. N = 861, no effect. Chabris, Heck, Mandart et al (2019). Social Psychology 50:127-132. doi:10.1027/1864-9335/a000361. abstract. r = -.03 and .02 against originals of r = .28 and .31.
- **Replication.** These are the replications. A multi-site registered test of stereotype threat in Black students (Forscher and others) was searched for and not found, so not established either way.
- **Rating.** contested for stereotype threat (negligible to small on real tests). strong that ego depletion is at most very small. strong that warmth priming doesn't replicate.
- **Would change (inference).** Don't justify cast decisions by stereotype threat; [M30] on interest and belonging is a different finding and stands. Don't justify pacing by "willpower runs out". Don't tell a director that small sensory cues will warm an audience.

## Q3. Flow

### P13. Flow is not one measured thing

- **Claim.** A 2012-2019 review found 108 flow-related constructs measured by 141 instruments, with mostly correlational applied work. A sport review calls the field near a "crisis point". A game pilot failed to separate flow from overload and boredom levels by self-report.
- **Citations.**

  - Livingston, Rosette, Washington (2012). Can an Agentic Black Woman Get Ahead? Psychological Science 23:354-358. doi:10.1177/0956797611428079. abstract.
  - Livingston, Pearce (2009). The Teddy-Bear Effect. Psychological Science 20:1229-1236. doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02431.x. abstract.
  - Galinsky, Hall, Cuddy (2013). Gendered Races. Psychological Science 24:498-506. doi:10.1177/0956797612457783. abstract.
  - Hall, Hall, Galinsky et al (2019). MOSAIC. Academy of Management Review 44:643-672. doi:10.5465/amr.2017.0109. abstract.
  - Hester, Payne, Brown-Iannuzzi et al (2020). On Intersectionality. Psychological Science 31:1013-1024. doi:10.1177/0956797620929979. abstract.
  - Biernat, Manis (1994). Shifting standards. JPSP 66:5-20. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.66.1.5. abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Livingston 2012: leader vignettes, sample not in abstract. Galinsky: six studies. Hester: simulations. Biernat: four studies; "objective" common-rule measures reduce the shift. Hall: theory.
- **Replication.** No direct replication seen; Hall notes Black women face less backlash yet worse outcomes.
- **Rating.** moderate that effects aren't additive. weak for any one pattern. no evidence in fiction: Search 6 returned no experiment varying a character's race with the narrative fixed.
- **Would change (inference).** A check of "assertive woman" alone will miss things. Rating a trait with an adjective hides the shift; check what the character does.

### P40. In fiction, character gender barely moved choice of story

- **Claim.** Randomising protagonist gender in matched short stories had almost no effect on which story adults chose to continue, with a small same-gender effect in women only. Demographic similarity did not raise identification or persuasion in two experiments.
- **Citations.**
  - Bologna, Lundberg, Wilkens (2025). Causal Effect of Character Gender on Readers' Preferences. Computational Humanities Research, Anthology of Computers and the Humanities 3:62-75. doi:10.63744/hEF2pUnudmPh. full (methods, results, discussion, limits).
  - Cohen, Weimann-Saks, Mazor-Tregerman (2017). Does Character Similarity Increase Identification and Persuasion? Media Psychology 21:506-528. doi:10.1080/15213269.2017.1302344. abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** **Fiction.** Bologna: 3,000 US Prolific adults, paired forced choice, two purpose-written short stories. Men +0.008 [-0.036, 0.051]; women +0.062 [0.017, 0.106]. One story pair; a choice, not enjoyment or judgement of traits; other gender identities excluded. Cohen: sex, nationality, age and residence varied.
- **Replication.** Not replicated; conflicts with low-powered earlier work the authors cite. [M33] has associations on balanced casts. Race: no equivalent found.
- **Rating.** moderate for gender in this one setting. no evidence for race.
- **Would change (inference).** Don't accept "players won't follow this protagonist" as a finding. Trait judgement is a separate question (38).

### P41. Tools exist for checking portrayals in text, and generated stories drift to stereotype

- **Claim.** A verb-frame method measures how much power and agency text gives a character. Generated stories link feminine characters to family and appearance and describe them as less powerful, even with high-power verbs in the prompt. Over 170 years, fiction's gender division has become less marked.
- **Citations.**
  - Sap, Prasettio, Holtzman et al (2017). Connotation Frames of Power and Agency in Modern Films. EMNLP 2329-2334. doi:10.18653/v1/D17-1247. abstract.
  - Lucy, Bamman (2021). Gender and Representation Bias in GPT-3 Generated Stories. Workshop on Narrative Understanding 48-55. doi:10.18653/v1/2021.nuse-1.5. abstract.
  - Underwood, Bamman, Lee (2018). The Transformation of Gender in English-Language Fiction. Journal of Cultural Analytics 3. doi:10.22148/16.019. abstract (partial).
- **Design, sample, effect.** Sap: film scripts, lexicon plus a web interface. Lucy: GPT-3, older than current models, topic models. Text measures, not audience responses.
- **Replication.** Lucy is one paper on an old model; [C78] is nearby: generated stories lean to tightly knit, positive relationships.
- **Rating.** moderate as description of text. no evidence that passing such a check changes audience response.
- **Would change (inference).** Useful on a generated draft, where the drift is a known risk: count who acts on whom, per character. It is a screen, not an answer.

## Q9. Ethics

### P42. Dark patterns work, mild ones don't provoke backlash, and knowing about them doesn't protect

- **Claim.** In representative US samples, mild dark patterns more than doubled sign-up to a dubious service, aggressive ones almost quadrupled it. Aggressive patterns produced backlash, mild ones didn't. Less educated people were more susceptible. Hidden information, trick questions and obstruction worked most. Users who recognised manipulation couldn't resist it.
- **Citations.**
  - Luguri, Strahilevitz (2021). Shining a Light on Dark Patterns. Journal of Legal Analysis 13:43-109. doi:10.1093/jla/laaa006. abstract.
  - Mathur, Acar, Friedman et al (2019). Dark Patterns at Scale. PACM HCI 3:1-32. doi:10.1145/3359183. abstract.
  - Gray, Kou, Battles et al (2018). The Dark (Patterns) Side of UX Design. CHI 1-14. doi:10.1145/3173574.3174108. abstract.
  - Bongard-Blanchy, Rossi, Rivas et al (2021). I am Definitely Manipulated. DIS 763-776. doi:10.1145/3461778.3462086. abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Luguri: two large experiments. First study, through the 2026-11-06 reviewer's fetch summary: N = 1,963, a fictitious plan with believed stakes, sign-up 11.3%, 25.8%, 41.9%; mood and anger showed no mild-condition backlash, while perceived freedom to refuse went 6.21 to 5.81 (significance not checked). Mathur: ~53K pages from ~11K shopping sites, 1,818 instances. Bongard-Blanchy: survey of 406. **Commerce, not learning or games.**
- **Replication.** Luguri replicated its own striking result within the paper.
- **Rating.** moderate to strong for commerce.
- **Would change (inference).** Player satisfaction won't reveal a mild dark pattern. That's why it needs review rather than playtest sentiment.

### P43. Engagement mechanics in games and learning apps: mainly surveys and interviews

- **Claim.** Players report daily rewards as motivation, as fear of missing out, or as chores. Streak displays raise engagement independently of actual past behaviour, by making the streak itself a goal. Duolingo users report fixating on gamification over learning. Autoplay undermines sense of agency. No empirical study of dark patterns in learning products was found.
- **Citations.**
  - Frommel, Mandryk (2022). Daily Quests or Daily Pests? PACM HCI 6:1-23. doi:10.1145/3549489. abstract.
  - Silverman, Barasch (2022). On or Off Track. Journal of Consumer Research 49:1095-1117. doi:10.1093/jcr/ucac029. abstract.
  - Hadi Mogavi, Guo, Zhang et al (2022). When Gamification Spoils Your Learning. L@S 175-188. doi:10.1145/3491140.3528274. abstract.
  - Lukoff, Lyngs, Zade et al (2021). How the Design of YouTube Influences User Sense of Agency. CHI 1-17. doi:10.1145/3411764.3445467. abstract.
  - Zagal, Björk, Lewis (2013). Dark Patterns in the Design of Games. https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/177148 . not opened (search summary).
- **Design, sample, effect.** Frommel: 178 participants, mixed methods. Silverman: seven studies, about the display, framed for firms' benefit. Mogavi: forum content analysis and 15 interviews; qualitative. Lukoff: survey of 120 and 13 co-design sessions.
- **Replication.** None direct.
- **Rating.** weak for harm. moderate that streak displays change behaviour. no evidence for dark patterns in learning products as a measured category.
- **Would change (inference).** A streak raises return without raising learning unless shown otherwise, so it falls under [L-C21] and [G-B1]. Report it with a delayed test.

### P44. Telling motivation from manipulation: conceptual criteria, one operational guide, no validated test

- **Claim.** Philosophy and HCI accounts define manipulation as hidden influence that exploits vulnerabilities, against a person's own interests. SDT gives an operational contrast: rationale, acknowledged feelings and choice, against pressure. Many nudges also shrink greatly at scale, so the case for "it works" is weaker than it seems.
- **Citations.**
  - Susser, Roessler, Nissenbaum (2019). Technology, autonomy, and manipulation. Internet Policy Review 8. doi:10.14763/2019.2.1410. abstract.
  - Mathur, Kshirsagar, Mayer (2021). What Makes a Dark Pattern... Dark? CHI 1-18. doi:10.1145/3411764.3445610. abstract.
  - Peters, Calvo, Ryan (2018). Designing for Motivation, Engagement and Wellbeing. Frontiers in Psychology 9. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00797. abstract.
  - Deci et al (1994), claim 2.
  - DellaVigna, Linos (2022). RCTs to Scale. Econometrica 90:81-116. doi:10.3982/ECTA18709. abstract.
  - Mertens, Herberz, Hahnel et al (2021). The effectiveness of nudging. PNAS 119. doi:10.1073/pnas.2107346118. abstract.
  - Maier, Bartoš, Stanley et al (2022). No evidence for nudging after adjusting for publication bias. PNAS 119. doi:10.1073/pnas.2200300119. abstract (title only).
- **Design, sample, effect.** DellaVigna: 126 RCTs, 23 million people; nudge units 1.4 percentage points (8.0%), academic papers 8.7 points (33.4%). The rest are conceptual.
- **Replication.** Maier directly disputes Mertens' pooled figure.
- **Rating.** weak: no empirical test distinguishes the two. moderate that nudge effects shrink at scale.
- **Would change (inference).** Two cases differ. **Consented fiction:** a mystery's misdirection is what the player came for and doesn't touch their time or money. **Product influence:** a mechanic that pulls them back or holds their attention is about their life outside the fiction. Test for the second, as a draft: would we still run it if the mechanism were shown on screen? Does the learner get something they'd endorse later? Does leaving cost anything? Is a delayed learning test the reason, or only return rate?

# Method and honesty notes

- **Search budget.** 9 of 10 web searches used, numbered as: 1 betrayal, 2 AI tutor tone, 3 stereotype threat replication, 4 needs and retention, 5 twist reinterpretation, 6 character gender and race, 7 dark patterns, 8 politeness, 9 protagonist attractiveness and aggression. No database search tools were used as a workaround. No search was refused.
- **Candidate DOIs came from my memory, and were confirmed or rejected only by Crossref exact lookup.** The check caught many errors, listed below. Nothing was rated on a paper I couldn't read at least in abstract, except where labelled not opened.
- **Abstract-level file.** Almost everything is `abstract`. Only Ballou and Bologna were read as text by the author; the top of this file says what an independent review opened. Sizes are given only where an abstract states them; many JPSP abstracts have none. Samples often went unreported in abstracts, and I've said so where it mattered.
- **Search results are not evidence here.** McLaren's numbers come from a search summary and are marked. Everything from a search summary is labelled not opened.
- **Candidates from memory carry selection risk.** I chose many papers I already knew of. The nulls were looked for deliberately, but the list isn't a systematic review. Anything said to be missing is "not found in this small pass", not "doesn't exist".
- **Grouping into a claim is my judgement.** The numbered claims combine separate papers; a reader should check an individual paper before quoting.
- **Ballou (2026) is a preprint**, and its author team overlaps with the scale it tests. I counted that as evidence against interest. Peer review not seen.
- **"Would change" lines** are mine, and no study tests them.
- **Not repeated.** Deci 1999, Howard 2021, Cerasoli 2014, Patall 2008, Lazowski 2016, Macnamara 2023, Burnette 2023, Sisk 2018, Yeager 2019, Kluger and DeNisi, Wisniewski, Metcalfe 2017, Ginns 2013, Carpenter 2013 (cited once as already held), from the library's evidence files; Grizzard 2023, Komeda 2017, Cheryan, Sherrick from `../story-design/EVIDENCE-MEASURED.md`.
