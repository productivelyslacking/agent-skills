# What measured research says makes people want to keep following a story

Evidence file, measured findings only (practitioner craft is in EVIDENCE-CRAFT.md). Research date 2026-11-06. No rules or skill text here. Cite an entry as `[M8]`; "claim 8" inside this file means M8.
The "Inference" lines were written with story games in mind, so check their fit for other forms. The findings are collected for games and interactive stories generally; genre and medium limits are named per claim.

## How to read this

**Opened.** `full` = I obtained the text and read the methods and results sections cited (not always every page). `abstract` = abstract only, through Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar or a repository page. `not opened` = I saw only a search summary, press piece, course notes or another paper's description; anything from it is labelled.

**Ratings.** strong = several consistent meta-analyses or large trials on this exact question. moderate = some good evidence with real limits. weak = thin, small, single-group or only correlational. contested = good evidence pointing in different directions. no evidence = I looked and found no measurement. not assessed = I didn't get far enough to judge. Where one claim covers two differently supported cases, each case is rated separately.

**Inference.** Every "Inference" line is my reasoning about writing. No study cited tested it unless the line says so.

**Age.** "Age" lines appear where the sample affects whether a finding applies to adults. "age not read" means the abstract gave none. Section "Which findings rest on whom" collects these.

**Population warning for the whole file.** Most experiments are psychology or communication undergraduates, on one sitting, with short stimuli and self-report just after. Almost nothing measures whether a person actually came back to a story days later. Treat that as the largest single gap.

**Not repeated from the library's evidence files** (`../teaching-design/EVIDENCE-LEARNING.md`, `../teaching-design/EVIDENCE-CODE-GAMES-AI.md`): Adams 2012, Clark 2016, Sýkora 2020, Jemmali 2018, Habgood 2011, Rowe 2011, seductive details, modality and redundancy, pedagogical-agent narration, Deci 1999, Sailer 2020, Andersen 2011 and 2012.

# 1. Wanting to continue

## M1. Curiosity peaks when people half-know the answer

- **Claim.** On trivia, curiosity follows an inverted U against confidence: highest at moderate confidence. When the information feels important, the peak shifts down toward very low to moderate confidence.
- **Citation.** Spitzer, Janz, Nie, Kiesel (2023). On the interplay of curiosity, confidence, and importance in knowing information. Psychological Research 88(1), 101-115. doi:10.1007/s00426-023-01841-9.
- **Opened.** abstract, plus the introduction and procedure in the PDF. Kang et al. 2009 and Dubey and Griffiths 2020 are named there; not opened.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Two experiments: Kang's own trivia material, then new COVID-19 material. Model comparison by BIC. No conventional effect size noted. Sample size not extracted.
- **Replication.** This paper is an independent replication of Kang et al. and says replications of curiosity findings are rare.
- **Rating.** moderate for trivia. weak for stories: nobody here tested a plot question.
- **Inference.** A question the audience can half-answer pulls harder than one they can't begin on, unless it matters to them; a stake may stand in for prior knowledge. Give the player enough to form a suspicion before asking them to care about the answer.

## M2. Creating and then resolving uncertainty feels better than being told at once, and people don't choose it

- **Claim.** Readers report a better experience when first teased with missing information and then given it than when given everything at once, yet when offered the choice they ask for it all at once.
- **Citation.** Ruan, Hsee, Lu (2018). The Teasing Effect: An Underappreciated Benefit of Creating and Resolving an Uncertainty. Journal of Marketing Research 55(4), 556-570. doi:10.1509/jmr.15.0346.
- **Opened.** abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Seven studies, including trivia reading and adverts. No effect sizes in the abstract; sample and age not read.
- **Replication.** Replicated across the paper's own seven studies. I saw no independent replication.
- **Rating.** moderate for trivia and adverts. weak for stories. no evidence for long-form play.
- **Inference.** Audience preference can't be the test of a withholding structure, because people say they want the answer now and enjoy it more later. The resolution is half the mechanism in this account, so teasing without payoff isn't what was measured.

## M3. Uncertainty raises curiosity, and curiosity is higher for good news

- **Claim.** In a lottery task, curiosity rose with outcome uncertainty and was higher for gains than losses; the two did not interact.
- **Citation.** van Lieshout, Traast, de Lange, Cools (2021). Curiosity or savouring? Information seeking is modulated by both uncertainty and valence. PLOS ONE 16(9), e0257011. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0257011.
- **Opened.** abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Lottery task, with uncertainty, expected value and valence manipulated independently. No effect size or sample read.
- **Replication.** none seen.
- **Rating.** weak for stories (a monetary task, not a narrative).
- **Inference.** Tentative: a question whose likely answer is bad news may pull less than one that could be good. Worth checking in play rather than assuming a darker mystery is automatically more gripping.

## M4. Transportation and engagement scales predict attitudes and enjoyment; none I found predicts coming back

- **Claim.** Validated scales of being absorbed in a story exist and correlate with enjoyment and story-consistent attitudes. The syntheses are about persuasion. I found no study using them to predict whether people return to a story.
- **Citations.**
  - van Laer, de Ruyter, Visconti, Wetzels (2014). The Extended Transportation-Imagery Model: A Meta-Analysis of the Antecedents and Consequences of Consumers' Narrative Transportation. Journal of Consumer Research 40(5), 797-817. doi:10.1086/673383. abstract. 132 effect sizes from 76 articles.
  - Busselle, Bilandzic (2009). Measuring Narrative Engagement. Media Psychology 12(4), 321-347. doi:10.1080/15213260903287259. abstract. Four dimensions: understanding, attentional focus, emotional engagement, presence. Film and TV viewers in two countries; the scale predicted enjoyment and story-consistent attitudes.
  - Yi, Liu, Walter, Busselle, Bilandzic (2026). Engagement Is Not One Thing: A Meta-Analytic Perspective on the Narrative Engagement Scale. Media Psychology, 1-25. doi:10.1080/15213269.2026.2705870. abstract. 52 studies, N = 12,705. Related to attitudes, intentions and behaviours consistent with the story. The attention and understanding dimensions linked most to less counterarguing; emotional engagement and presence to transportation and identification. Note two of the authors built the scale.
  - Braddock, Dillard (2016). Meta-analytic evidence for the persuasive effect of narratives on beliefs, attitudes, intentions, and behaviors. Communication Monographs 83(4), 446-467. doi:10.1080/03637751.2015.1128555. abstract. Beliefs r = .17 (k = 37), attitudes r = .19 (k = 40), intentions r = .17 (k = 28), behaviours r = .23 (k = 5). Unidentified moderators remain.
  - Oschatz, Marker (2020). Long-term Persuasive Effects in Narrative Communication Research: A Meta-Analysis. Journal of Communication 70(4), 473-496. doi:10.1093/joc/jqaa017. abstract. 14 studies; a story beats non-story on attitudes and intentions at immediate and delayed measurement.
- **Replication.** Several meta-analyses agree on the persuasion link.
- **Rating.** strong that absorption and story-consistent attitudes go together. no evidence that a scale score predicts continuation.
- **Inference.** A playtest absorption score tells you the player was in the story in that session. Don't report it as evidence they'll return. Use the scale as a diagnostic alongside actual return counts.

## M5. In a game, flow and narrative engagement may not be separate things when measured

- **Claim.** In two game-based experiments, flow and narrative engagement correlated nearly perfectly, so the measures may not be statistically distinct.
- **Citation.** Sherrick (2020). Empirically comparing flow, narrative engagement, and enjoyment as responses to a computer game. Atlantic Journal of Communication 29(4), 230-245. doi:10.1080/15456870.2020.1755289.
- **Opened.** abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Two experiments; sample not read.
- **Replication.** none seen.
- **Rating.** weak.
- **Inference.** A high narrative engagement score in a game may be partly saying the play felt good, so it can't credit the writing alone. To credit the story, compare against a story-thin build rather than reading the scale.

## M6. Character bonds are not a lonely person's substitute, and they go with similarity and involvement

- **Claim.** Across four decades of work, parasocial relationships did not significantly relate to social deficiencies. They correlated strongly with homophily, identification and transportation, and predicted media involvement more strongly than momentary parasocial interaction did.
- **Citations.**
  - Tukachinsky, Walter, Saucier (2020). Antecedents and Effects of Parasocial Relationships: A Meta-Analysis. Journal of Communication 70(6), 868-894. doi:10.1093/joc/jqaa034. abstract. k = 120. The abstract gives no coefficients and no breakdown by medium, so I can't say how much of it is games.
  - Risco, Mills (2026). Interpersonal and Gaming Behaviors: Contrasting Players With and Without Parasocial Relationships with Fictional Characters. Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking 29(8), 577-583. doi:10.1177/21522715261461616. abstract. 584 gamers from Reddit, mean age 24.98, 81.6% male. Players with such bonds did not differ on relatedness frustration but reported more interpersonal difficulties.
  - Milman, Mills (2023). The relationship between parasocial friendship quality with non-playable video game characters, gaming motivations, and obsessive vs harmonious passion. Telematics and Informatics Reports 10, 100057. doi:10.1016/j.teler.2023.100057. abstract. Friendship quality with an NPC related to social, fantasy, escape and coping motives; correlational.
  - Rain, Mar (2021). Adult attachment and engagement with fictional characters. Journal of Social and Personal Relationships 38(9), 2792-2813. doi:10.1177/02654075211018513. abstract. Anxiety and avoidance predict different forms of character engagement; Study 2 preregistered. Directions not in the abstract.
- **Replication.** The meta-analysis is the summary; game work is new and survey-only.
- **Rating.** moderate for the non-compensation finding. weak for games. no evidence that a stronger bond causes players to continue: everything here is correlational, and I found no experiment that manipulates the bond and measures return.
- **Inference.** Write the cast for similarity and shared situation rather than assuming a lonely audience. Treat "the player will come back for this character" as a hypothesis to measure.

## M7. Audiences want liked characters to win, and this explains the ending better than the whole story

- **Claim.** In a preregistered three-act written narrative, moral behaviour created positive dispositions, which predicted hope for good outcomes, and dispositions combined with outcomes predicted affect and enjoyment. The theory explained liking of the ending better than overall enjoyment or appreciation. Participants also wanted liked moral characters to act immorally if it raised their chance of a good outcome.
- **Citation.** Grizzard, Francemone, Frazer, Fitzgerald, Monge, Henry (2023). A comprehensive experimental test of the affective disposition theory of drama. Journal of Communication 73(4), 289-303. doi:10.1093/joc/jqac053.
- **Opened.** abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Preregistered 2 x 2 x 2 between-subjects. Sample not read. No effect sizes in the abstract.
- **Replication.** Tests a theory with an older record (Zillmann 1975 and 1977 are named in Schibler's references; not opened). Not itself replicated as far as I saw.
- **Rating.** moderate. Written text, one sitting.
- **Inference.** Establish what the protagonist does for others before asking the audience to hope. A morally grey act from an already-liked lead costs less than from a stranger. This speaks to how the ending lands, less to whether people keep going.

## M8. Cliffhangers: desire for the next part rises against a resolved ending, but not against a merely open one

- **Claim.** Compared with a resolved ending, a cliffhanger raised desire for a further installment without costing enjoyment. Compared with an ending that left the story unfinished but not at a peak, a cliffhanger raised arousal and did not raise enjoyment or the intention to continue.
- **Citations.**
  - Schibler, Hahn, Green (2023). Investigating Responses to Narrative Cliffhangers Using Affective Disposition Theory. Media Psychology 27(1), 1-25. doi:10.1080/15213269.2023.2219456. abstract. Two between-subjects experiments, N = 202 and N = 273, written stories. Cliffhanger readers wanted a further installment more than protagonist-win readers (both studies) and antagonist-win readers (Study 2). No significant differences in enjoyment or suspense.
  - Wirz, Ort, Rasch, Fahr (2023). The role of cliffhangers in serial entertainment: An experiment on cliffhangers' effects on enjoyment, arousal, and intention to continue watching. Psychology of Popular Media 12(2), 186-196. doi:10.1037/ppm0000392. full (accepted manuscript). N = 133 (93 women, 40 men), aged 18 to 30, mean 21.06, Swiss university; 3 to 4 episodes, about 2 h 49 min, of real drama series that participants had not seen, ending with or without a cliffhanger, against a documentary control. Self-reported arousal: cliffhanger higher than no cliffhanger, p = .003. Skin conductance: not significant, p = .40. Cortisol: overall p = .06, cliffhanger vs no cliffhanger contrast p = .02. Enjoyment and intention to continue: no difference between versions. Drama beat documentary on both.
- **Limits.** Wirz measured intention to continue "at some point", after a long session, and say the suspense they induced was fairly low and that cliffhangers may matter more for continuing right away. Schibler measured desire, in written text, not behaviour. Neither measured real continuation. Their control endings differ, which I think is the main reason they can look contradictory.
- **Replication.** No direct replication of either found.
- **Rating.** contested for intention. moderate that a cliffhanger doesn't hurt enjoyment. no evidence on real adult return behaviour.
- **Age.** Wirz is young adults only. Schibler's ages not read.
- **Inference.** The openness may be doing the work rather than the peak moment. Leaving a question unresolved at the end of a part is the supported move; a heightened moment on top of it hasn't been shown to add pull, at least across a gap. Claim 9 doesn't separate them: its serial episodes often ended on cliffhangers.

## M9. A serial structure sustained play where self-contained episodes declined (children only)

- **Claim.** Children given a serial story during an active video game kept playing and moving at later visits, while those given self-contained episodes declined. Self-reported immersion did not differ.
- **Citation.** Lu, Green, Sousa, Hwang, Lee, Thompson et al. (2023). To Pause With a Cliffhanger or a Temporary Closure? The Differential Impact of Serial Versus Episodic Narratives on Children's Physical Activity Behaviors. Communication Research 52(5), 600-625. doi:10.1177/00936502231166091.
- **Opened.** full, through a page-extraction tool of the PMC version (one table row flagged garbled by the tool and not used).
- **Design, sample, effect.** Randomised, N = 44 (22 per group), aged 8 to 12, one Boston-area school, four visits over about 3 weeks, Kinect game with a 12-episode animation. Gameplay duration: visit x condition p = .04; episodic fell from 12.8 to 8.9 min, serial stayed near 11 to 12 min. Visit 4 moderate-to-vigorous activity (hip): serial 8.2 vs episodic 6.3 min, p = .01. Immersion no difference (p = .11). Overall condition effects in the multilevel models were not significant; the differences appear at later visits.
- **Limits.** Not preregistered, one school, after-school setting, small. Visits were scheduled by the study and each child received a $100 gift card at the end, so attendance wasn't an outcome; the measure is self-chosen play minutes per visit. Authors flag possible ceiling on immersion and weak self-report under age 11.
- **Replication.** none found.
- **Rating.** weak. It is, however, the only study I found that measures real behaviour across repeat visits for this question.
- **Age.** Children only. Do not read as adult evidence.
- **Inference.** Behaviour moved where self-report did not, so asking players how immersed they were may miss the effect a serial structure has on return. Build the measure on return, not on a survey.

## M10. People report binge-watching as driven by habit first, with suspense and anticipation also significant

- **Claim.** In a survey, frequency of binge-watching was motivated largely by entrenched habit, with suspense and anticipation and emotion regulation also significant.
- **Citation.** Rubenking, Bracken (2018). Binge-Watching: A Suspenseful, Emotional, Habit. Communication Research Reports 35(5), 381-391. doi:10.1080/08824096.2018.1525346.
- **Opened.** abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Survey, N = 797, college and representative adult samples. Self-report, cross-sectional.
- **Replication.** none seen.
- **Rating.** weak.
- **Inference.** A regular release slot or return ritual may matter as much as story hooks. Not tested for games.

## M11. Zeigarnik: no general memory advantage for unfinished things; Ovsiankina: people do tend to resume

- **Claim.** A 2025 meta-analysis found interrupted tasks recalled about as well as finished ones, but a general tendency to resume interrupted tasks, about 67% of the time against a 50% chance rate.
- **Citation.** Ghibellini, Meier (2025). Interruption, recall and resumption: a meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects. Humanities and Social Sciences Communications 12, 962. doi:10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w.
- **Opened.** full.
- **Design, sample, effect.** Ratio of mean recall for interrupted to completed tasks: 0.99, excluding Zeigarnik's original (37 publications). The ratio Zeigarnik herself used, which the authors say overestimates and is sensitive to extremes: 1.09 without her result, 1.13 with (6 and 7 publications). Interrupted share of total recall: 49.16% to 49.43%. Cohen's dz = 0.15 (8 publications). Resumption: 66.79% to 67.00% (20 to 21 publications). Mostly students in older lab studies: my count of sample labels in the extracted tables gives Students 38, Children 6, Adults 1 (a rough count from text extraction, by study row). The authors suggest the memory effect may depend on conditions such as experimenter authority and task involvement, more common historically. I found no confidence intervals, preregistration or publication-bias analysis in the extracted text.
- **Replication.** The paper exists because replication has been hard; Van Bergen 1968 is the older review (named there, not opened).
- **Rating.** moderate against a general memory effect. moderate for resumption of lab tasks. no evidence in stories: no included study I noted, and no story study I found, tests either effect in narrative. The cliffhanger papers above cite the idea but do not test it.
- **Inference.** Stop justifying cliffhangers by "people remember unfinished things better". If interruption pulls people back, the supported account is a tendency to finish what they started, which may need them to have started something of their own, such as an unsolved case. That application to stories is mine.

## M12. A story people want can pull them into another activity, and the pull fades

- **Claim.** Holding page-turner audiobooks at the gym raised visits at first, and the effect declined. A large later trial found that giving audiobooks plus encouragement to combine them with exercise raised weekly workouts, with lasting effect.
- **Citations.**
  - Milkman, Minson, Volpp (2014). Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling. Management Science 60(2), 283-299. doi:10.1287/mnsc.2013.1784. abstract. Field experiment. Initially 51% (gym-only access) and 29% (encouragement) more visits than control, declining over time, especially after Thanksgiving. 61% later paid for gym-only access. Kirgios gives its size as N = 226.
  - Kirgios, Mandel, Park, Milkman, Gromet, Kay et al. (2020). Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: A field experiment. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 161, 20-35. doi:10.1016/j.obhdp.2020.09.003. abstract and introduction. N = 6,792, four-week programme. Audiobook plus encouragement: 10 to 14% higher likelihood of a weekly workout and 10 to 12% more workouts, during and up to seventeen weeks after. Encouragement on top of audiobook alone added modestly.
- **Replication.** Replicated in direction at much larger scale by the same research group, with a different design.
- **Rating.** moderate for exercise. weak as evidence about story: I did not read that audiobooks had to be serial or fiction in the 2020 trial, and the mechanism is pairing, not plot.
- **Age.** Age not read for either trial.
- **Inference.** Direct precedent for "story as the reason to do the hard thing": plausibly the nearest measured evidence for using a story to pull people into a harder activity. The fading effect is a warning that pull is not permanent. No study tests this for practice inside a game.

# 2. Games

## M13. Does story raise enjoyment, persistence or retention in games? Not shown

- **Claim.** No meta-analysis of story vs no story on enjoyment or retention in entertainment games was found. Single studies are mixed: story raised identification, presence and arousal in a shooter; another study found story and character information changed nothing about identification; a correlational study ties story to appreciation and gameplay to enjoyment.
- **Citations.**
  - Schneider et al. (2004; Crossref's record lists only Schneider, co-authors not confirmed). Death with a Story: How Story Impacts Emotional, Motivational, and Physiological Responses to First-Person Shooter Video Games. Human Communication Research 30(3), 361-375. doi:10.1093/hcr/30.3.361. abstract. With story, greater identification, presence and physiological arousal; no change in self-reported arousal or dominance. No N in the abstract. The abstract says it "clearly demonstrates" players enjoy story, which goes further than the measures it lists.
  - Hitchens, Drachen, Richards (2012). An investigation of player to player character identification via personal pronouns. Proceedings of the 8th Australasian Conference on Interactive Entertainment, 1-11. doi:10.1145/2336727.2336738. abstract. Pronoun use as the measure. Story and information about the player character had no effect; player gender and game experience did. Sample not read.
  - Oliver, Bowman, Woolley, Rogers, Sherrick, Chung (2016). Video games as meaningful entertainment experiences. Psychology of Popular Media Culture 5(4), 390-405. doi:10.1037/ppm0000066. abstract. N = 512, recall of a fun or a meaningful game. Enjoyment went most with gameplay, competence and autonomy; appreciation most with story, insight and relatedness. Recall, correlational within condition.
  - Elson, Breuer, Ivory, Quandt (2014). More Than Stories With Buttons: Narrative, Mechanics, and Context as Determinants of Player Experience in Digital Games. Journal of Communication 64(3), 521-542. doi:10.1111/jcom.12096. abstract. A theoretical argument, not data.
  - Lin (2025), University of Hawaii, Digital Narrative in Puzzle Video Games. https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/f12e0ed0-6995-4eff-a975-19ea715484da. not opened (repository blocked the fetch; search summary only): N = 81, narrative raised enjoyment but not motivation to continue.
  - Schwarz, Cardon, Chastin, Stragier, De Marez et al. (2021). Does Dynamic Tailoring of A Narrative-Driven Exergame Result in Higher User Engagement among Adolescents? Results from A Cluster-Randomized Controlled Trial. International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health 18(14), 7444. doi:10.3390/ijerph18147444. abstract. N = 94, mean age 14.61. Engagement low in both arms; tailoring raised narrative sensation but the non-tailored arm played longer.
- **Replication.** none.
- **Rating.** contested for identification and arousal. weak for enjoyment. no evidence for retention in adult entertainment games.
- **Age.** Schwarz is adolescents. Others age not read.
- **Inference.** "Story keeps players going" is an assumption in games research as well as in the learning-game work already in the library. It can stay as a hypothesis, tested with a story-thin build and a survival curve.

## M14. Suspense raises game enjoyment, in one small experiment

- **Claim.** A first-person shooter manipulated to high suspense was enjoyed more than a low-suspense version.
- **Citation.** Klimmt, Rizzo, Vorderer, Koch, Fischer (2009). Experimental Evidence for Suspense as Determinant of Video Game Enjoyment. CyberPsychology & Behavior 12(1), 29-31. doi:10.1089/cpb.2008.0060.
- **Opened.** abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** 63 participants, randomised, 10-item enjoyment scale. No effect size in the abstract.
- **Replication.** none seen.
- **Rating.** weak.
- **Inference.** Direction fits film work; the suspense was action suspense in a shooter, so it says little about mystery plotting.

## M15. Real play data: total playtime has a common shape and much of what's bought goes unfinished, but nothing separates story from non-story

- **Claim.** Total playtime follows one distribution across thousands of games. Across Steam, most owned games are little played and achievement completion is low. I found no published data on where players stop inside a story.
- **Citations.**
  - Bauckhage, Kersting, Sifa, Thurau, Drachen, Canossa (2012). How players lose interest in playing a game: An empirical study based on distributions of total playing times. IEEE CIG, 139-146. doi:10.1109/cig.2012.6374148. abstract. Over 250,000 players, five action-adventure and shooter games; Weibull fits all five.
  - Sifa, Bauckhage, Drachen (2014). The Playtime Principle: Large-scale cross-games interest modeling. IEEE CIG, 1-8. doi:10.1109/cig.2014.6932906. abstract. Over 6 million players, over 3,000 Steam games, over 5 billion hours; the same Weibull model applies.
  - O'Neill, Vaziripour, Wu, Zappala (2016). Condensing Steam. Internet Measurement Conference, 81-95. doi:10.1145/2987443.2987489. full (cited sections). 108.7 million accounts, 384.3 million owned games. Share of owned games unplayed: Action 41.49%, Indie 32.3%, Strategy 28.86%, RPG 24.26%. Median average achievement completion: 11% single player, 12% multiplayer; mode 5%. Half did not play in the two-week window measured.
- **Limits.** Achievements are a proxy for progress and the design of achievements varies. No telemetry study here splits games by amount of story. Bundle purchases may inflate the unplayed count (my guess, not measured here). Data are about a decade old. Age not available.
- **Replication.** Descriptive results repeat across the two Weibull papers (shared authors).
- **Rating.** moderate as description of Steam-era play (very large data, but one platform, shared authors on the Weibull papers, abstracts only for those two). The abstracts show a common distribution, not by themselves that players stop early. no evidence that any of it differs for story games.
- **Inference.** Low completion is common across games, so a high stop rate in a story game isn't by itself evidence the story failed. A survival curve needs a comparison, such as the game's own earlier builds.

## M16. Dialogue and cutscene skip rates: unanswered

- **Status.** Unanswered. One search for telemetry on cutscene and dialogue skipping found no published rate.
- **What turned up.** Kojima said in an interview, reported by FRVR on 13 August 2025 (https://frvr.com/blog/news/hideo-kojima-says-a-lot-of-people-skipped-death-stranding-cutscenes-but-they-are-part-of-the-game/, opened, press report of an IndieWire interview not opened), that his stats showed many players stopped watching Death Stranding's cutscenes. He gave no number. Vendor pages (getbruin.com) list skip rate as a metric with no data; not evidence.
- **Rating.** no evidence.
- **Inference.** If the project logs its own skip and completion per beat, it will hold data the literature doesn't. Studios likely hold this privately; absence here doesn't mean no one knows.

## M17. Agency: felt agency survives some removal of branching, but feedback was not shown to be the cause; effects on engagement are mostly null

- **Claim.** Readers of a branching story and of a non-branching version with choice-specific feedback reported similar agency on most items, but branching still beat feedback on the item about whether the story would have differed. Branching beat a minimal-feedback version on every item. The study's own second hypothesis failed: feedback vs minimal differed on one item only. Choices between visibly different outcomes felt more agentic. Experiments adding interactivity to a story often found no difference in engagement or transportation.
- **Citations.**
  - Fendt, Harrison, Ware, Cardona-Rivera, Roberts (2012). Achieving the Illusion of Agency. ICIDS 2012, 114-125. doi:10.1007/978-3-642-34851-8_11. full. Text choose-your-own-adventure. Branching n = 79 (52 analysed on the matched path), non-branching with feedback n = 54, non-branching minimal n = 44; mean ages 25 to 28, snowball sample. Branching beat minimal on every item (p = .003 to .032). Branching vs feedback: significant on one of six items, "the story would have been different if I had selected different choices" (p = .030), and marginal on control over the story (p = .084). Feedback vs minimal: significant on only one of six ("my actions were important", p = .039), so the second hypothesis failed. Gender mix differed sharply between conditions (men/women: 20/32, 47/7, 33/11). Many pairwise tests, and I saw no correction.
  - Cardona-Rivera, Robertson, Ware, Harrison, Roberts, Young (2014). Foreseeing Meaningful Choices. AIIDE 10(1), 9-15. doi:10.1609/aiide.v10i1.12716. full (cited sections). n = 88, 18 or older, mean 27.5, SD 10.42; 95% had played games for more than five years, 63% called themselves gamers; unpaid online sample. Choices leading to situationally different content were rated higher in agency; intra-class checks were not all clean.
  - Steinemann, Iten, Opwis, Forde, Frasseck, Mekler (2017). Interactive Narratives Affecting Social Change. Journal of Media Psychology 29(1), 54-66. doi:10.1027/1864-1105/a000211. abstract. Yoked design, 634 participants: no significant difference in prosocial behaviour between interactive and noninteractive versions.
  - Ferchaud, Oliver (2019). It's my choice: The effects of moral decision-making on narrative game engagement. Journal of Gaming & Virtual Worlds 11(2), 101-118. doi:10.1386/jgvw.11.2.101_1. abstract. Modified Fallout: New Vegas. Almost nobody chose the bad option, so that cell was dropped. No differences in transportation or parasocial interaction; those who committed more moral actions identified less with the main character. Those choosing good felt more moral than those forced to.
  - Green, Jenkins (2020). Need for Cognition, Transportability, and Engagement with Interactive Narratives. Games for Health Journal 9(3), 182-186. doi:10.1089/g4h.2019.0095. abstract. Two studies, linear vs interactive. Need for cognition linked to more immersion in the interactive version, on different measures in each study; a transportability effect did not replicate.
  - Felnhofer, Wittmann, Reichmann, König-Teshnizi, Kothgassner (2022). Character identification is predicted by narrative transportation, immersive tendencies, and interactivity. Current Psychology 42(22), 18567-18577. doi:10.1007/s12144-022-03048-4. abstract. N = 228, aged 15 to 65, purported interactivity. Interactivity predicted identification; age did not.
  - Walter, Murphy, Gillig (2017). To Walk a Mile in Someone Else's Shoes: How Narratives Can Change Causal Attribution Through Story Exploration and Character Customization. Human Communication Research 44(1), 31-57. doi:10.1093/hcre.12112. abstract. N = 163, 116, 230. Exploration raised identification and engagement, and with customisation had the strongest effect on attribution.
  - Sridharan, Villa, Denisova, Pirker (2025). Exploring the (Placebo) Effect of an AI-Powered Dialogue System on Player Experience. IEEE Conference on Games, 1-8. doi:10.1109/cog64752.2025.11114156. abstract. Murder-mystery game. Being told a narrative was human-written raised experience, immersion and perceived autonomy regardless of what was actually played; AI dialogue itself raised perceived autonomy. Sample not read.
- **Replication.** Same research group for the first two. Green's own replication attempt partly failed.
- **Rating.** weak that real branching is unnecessary for felt agency. contested that choice raises engagement. no evidence on continuation.
- **Age.** Fendt and Cardona-Rivera are adults in their twenties and up. Felnhofer found no age effect from 15 to 65.
- **Inference.** Don't conclude illusory choice is free: the "feedback alone preserves it" reading is stronger than this data allows, and players did notice that the story would not have changed. What is better supported: choices must offer visibly different situations, and where branching can't be afforded, spend writing on that difference. Ferchaud's dropped cell also suggests a bad option is mostly not taken, so authoring it may be low value unless the story forces it. Expectation about authorship can matter more than the text (Sridharan).

## M18. Voice acting: no direct measurement found

- **Claim.** I found no experiment comparing voiced with text-only character dialogue on immersion, enjoyment or continuation in games.
- **Closest.**
  - Kao, Ratan, Mousas, Magana (2021). The Effects of a Self-Similar Avatar Voice in Educational Games. Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction 5(CHI PLAY), 1-28. doi:10.1145/3474665. abstract. 2 x 3 study in a Java programming game; a voice similar to the player's own raised performance, time spent, identification, competence, relatedness and immersion. Sample not read. This is about the player's avatar sounding like them, not about voice acting.
  - Ahrens-Schwabe, Kosch, Boomgaarden, Stocker (2026). First- or Third-Person: The Impact of Narrative Voice and Reception Mode on Empathy, Identification, and Parasocial Interaction in Literary Engagement. Empirical Studies of the Arts. doi:10.1177/02762374261451144. abstract. Novella: audiobook reduced self-oriented emotions (empathic stress and emotional involvement) relative to print; no other significant effects. Not a game.
- **Searched.** One search on voice acting and immersion (press, forums, vendor claims, and a voice-input VR study; none counted). Citation lists of Kao and Birk.
- **Replication.** none.
- **Rating.** no evidence for voice acting. weak for the adjacent results.
- **Inference.** The library's existing evidence on narration (C24 in EVIDENCE-LEARNING.md) covers learning. For wanting to continue, the case for voicing rests on craft, accessibility or hypothesis. It should be budgeted as a bet and tested, for instance by comparing retention in a text-first build.

# 3. Mystery and surprise

## M19. Suspense and surprise structures both beat an event list; unresolved endings read as less of a story

- **Claim.** In structural-affect work, liking ratings were lowest for a plain event sequence and higher for a suspense or surprise arrangement of the same events; a version with the ending omitted scored lower on being "a story".
- **Citations.**
  - Brewer, Lichtenstein (1981), Event schemas, story schemas, and story grammars, in Attention and Performance IX, 363-379. not opened. Numbers from course notes: https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/694Empirical/Brewer_Lichtenstein_1981.htm. Liking: base 2.15, suspense 4.85, foreshadowed suspense 4.30, surprise 4.30, suspense with outcome revealed first 3.65, no resolution 3.85. "Is a story": base 2.45, suspense 5.05, no resolution 2.75. Sample not given in the notes.
  - Brewer, Lichtenstein (1982). Stories are to entertain: A structural-affect theory of stories. Journal of Pragmatics 6(5-6), 473-486. doi:10.1016/0378-2166(82)90021-2. not opened; bibliographic record only.
  - Hoeken, van Vliet (2000). Suspense, curiosity, and surprise: How discourse structure influences the affective and cognitive processing of a story. Poetics 27(4), 277-286. doi:10.1016/s0304-422x(99)00021-2. not opened; bibliographic record only. I can't report its result.
- **Replication.** Later work uses the theory; I did not confirm a direct replication.
- **Rating.** weak (one old stimulus known only through notes, unknown sample).
- **Inference.** Order is a free lever: the same events reordered change how they are liked. No study here picks suspense over surprise as a general winner. The suspense vs surprise question, for long play, is unanswered.

## M20. Mystery enjoyment rises with uncertainty about the culprit; how an ending lands depends on the reader

- **Claim.** Readers enjoyed a mystery more while highly uncertain about the culprit and curious. After the resolution, high self-esteem readers disliked a confirming ending and low self-esteem readers disliked a surprising one.
- **Citation.** Knobloch-Westerwick, Keplinger (2006). Mystery Appeal: Effects of Uncertainty and Resolution on the Enjoyment of Mystery. Media Psychology 8(3), 193-212. doi:10.1207/s1532785xmep0803_1.
- **Opened.** abstract. Press release (news.osu.edu) says German students; not otherwise confirmed.
- **Design, sample, effect.** N = 84, one short story, low or high uncertainty, three resolution types. No effect sizes in the abstract.
- **Replication.** I scanned works citing it, filtering titles for mystery, detective, crime and surprise; none looks like a replication. Not an exhaustive check.
- **Rating.** weak. A self-esteem split in 84 people deserves caution.
- **Inference.** Keep more than one suspect plausible for as long as possible; that half of the finding is cheap to apply. Don't plan an ending around the self-esteem result.

## M21. Twist endings: reading behaviour measured, enjoyment and fair foreshadowing not

- **Claim.** Readers who empathised more and held stronger expectations read twist stories more slowly the first time and faster the second. No study I found tests whether foreshadowing makes a twist more satisfying.
- **Citation.** Komeda, Taira, Tsunemi, Kusumi, Rapp (2017). A sixth sense. Scientific Study of Literature 7(2), 203-231. doi:10.1075/ssol.17002.kom.
- **Opened.** abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** 28 Japanese readers, then 36 English speakers. Correlations with reading times.
- **Replication.** Experiment 2 extended Experiment 1 across languages.
- **Rating.** weak. no evidence on fair foreshadowing: one search returned craft opinion only.
- **Inference.** Fair play in mysteries is a craft claim. Keep it, labelled as craft.

## M22. Spoilers: the "spoilers help" finding does not replicate reliably; effects are small and inconsistent

- **Claim.** A widely reported 2011 result that spoiled stories are enjoyed more was followed by replications finding harm or nothing. Across the record the effects look small, inconsistent and dependent on type and medium.
- **Citations.**
  - Leavitt, Christenfeld (2011). Story Spoilers Don't Spoil Stories. Psychological Science 22(9), 1152-1154. doi:10.1177/0956797611417007. abstract, which contains no results; result from secondary descriptions: three experiments, undergraduates, twist, mystery and literary stories, spoiled enjoyed more, and only when the spoiler was a separate paragraph (OnFiction, https://www.onfiction.ca/2011/08/research-bulletin-do-spoilers-spoil.html).
  - Johnson, Rosenbaum (2015). Spoiler Alert. Communication Research 42(8), 1068-1088. doi:10.1177/0093650214564051. abstract. Within-subjects, N = 412: unspoiled more fun, suspenseful, moving and enjoyable.
  - Levine, Betzner, Autry (2016). The Effect of Spoilers on the Enjoyment of Short Stories. Discourse Processes 53(7), 513-531. doi:10.1080/0163853x.2016.1141350. abstract. Close replication: spoilers before a story reduced enjoyment; mid-story no effect.
  - Daniel, Katz (2018). Spoilers Affect the Enjoyment of Television Episodes but Not Short Stories. Psychological Reports 122(5), 1794-1807. doi:10.1177/0033294118793971. abstract. Negative for TV under some conditions, not for short stories.
  - Johnson, Rosenbaum (2017). (Don't) Tell Me How It Ends: Spoilers, Enjoyment, and Involvement in Television and Film. Media Psychology 21(4), 582-612. doi:10.1080/15213269.2017.1338964. abstract. "Quite small and qualified" effects; spoilers trigger reactance about upcoming films; beneficial for faithful adaptations.
  - Johnson, Udvardi, Eden, Rosenbaum (2020). Spoilers Go Bump in the Night. Journal of Media Psychology 32(1), 14-25. doi:10.1027/1864-1105/a000252. abstract. Horror film scenes: no main effects; need for affect moderated small plot-point spoilers.
  - Levine, Raines (2025). The Role of Different Spoiler Types in the Experience of Short Stories. Scientific Study of Literature 14(1), 27-44. doi:10.61645/ssol.190. full (cited sections). College students, n = 326, preregistered, target n = 300, published twist stories, full and thematic spoilers, with delay manipulated. Enjoyment F(2, 579.5) = 1.25, p = .29. No significant effect of type or delay.
  - Rosenbaum, Johnson (2016). Who's afraid of spoilers? Need for cognition, need for affect, and narrative selection and enjoyment. Psychology of Popular Media Culture 5(3), 273-289. doi:10.1037/ppm0000076. not opened; bibliographic record only, no abstract retrievable. Its title names need for cognition and need for affect. Daniel and Katz's abstract says other research ties spoiler results to need for cognition, without naming this paper there.
  - Yan, Tsang (2015). The misforecasted spoiler effect: Underlying mechanism and boundary conditions. Journal of Consumer Psychology 26(1), 81-90. doi:10.1016/j.jcps.2015.05.003. abstract. People overpredict harm from outcome spoilers, and underpredict it when the spoiler reveals the process of the plot.
  - Ryoo, Wang, Lu (2020). Do Spoilers Really Spoil? Using Topic Modeling to Measure the Effect of Spoiler Reviews on Box Office Revenue. Journal of Marketing 85(2), 70-88. doi:10.1177/0022242920937703. abstract. Observational panel with instruments plus experiments: spoiler intensity elasticity .06 on revenue, read as reducing uncertainty about quality. This is about whether to go, not enjoyment.
  - Zhou, Chai, Zhang, Zuo, Liu (2026). The effects of spoiler types on audience immersion: an inter-subject correlation study of heart rate. Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications 11(1). doi:10.1186/s41235-026-00733-x. abstract. N = 76 students; a video spoiler raised narrative engagement relative to other conditions.
- **Replication.** Direct replication attempts disagree. Levine and Raines is preregistered, the closest to that standard. I did not find a registered multi-lab replication, or any spoiler experiment in a game.
- **Rating.** contested on direction. moderate that any average effect is small. no evidence for games or puzzles where the player must solve the thing spoiled.
- **Age.** Mostly college students; adequate for adults, weaker for older adults.
- **Inference.** Both popular claims should go. A story shouldn't rest only on a twist staying hidden, because players will be spoiled and the harm isn't large. But a mystery game is different: a spoiled solution removes the player's task, not just the surprise, and this literature doesn't cover it. Yan and Tsang's process result suggests protecting how the mystery unfolds, not only the culprit's name.

## M23. Unreliable narrators: small field, no enjoyment or retention result found

- **Claim.** Whether unreliable narration raises engagement is not known. Perspective affected trust in one study: readers trusted a potentially unreliable character more under third-person narration than first-person, and empathy did not differ.
- **Citations.**
  - van Lissa, Caracciolo, van Duuren, van Leuveren (2016). Difficult Empathy. The Effect of Narrative Perspective on Readers' Engagement with a First-Person Narrator. Diegesis 5(1), 43-63. https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/difficult-empathy-the-effect-of-narrative-perspective-on-readers-/. abstract. 76 Dutch high-school students, one story.
  - van de Ven (2025), Trust in Stories: A Reader Response Study of (Un)Reliability in Akutagawa's "In a Grove". Literature 5(4), 24. https://www.mdpi.com/2410-9789/5/4/24. not opened (search summary): exploratory, readers sometimes contradict narratologists' expectations.
- **Replication.** none.
- **Rating.** no evidence for effects on enjoyment or continuation. weak for the trust result.
- **Age.** van Lissa is high-school students only.
- **Inference.** A first-person narrator who may be lying has no measured benefit or cost for wanting to continue. If first-person lowers trust, that may help a suspicious-narrator twist and hurt a narrator meant to be trusted; this is untested.

## M24. Do audiences enjoy solving ahead of the detective? Unanswered

- **Status.** No direct measurement found. One search on guessing the ending and one citation scan of Mystery Appeal.
- **Closest.**
  - Claim 20: guessing right pleased some readers and not others; self-esteem-split, small study.
  - Kärnä, Väkevä, Rautalahti, Lindqvist (2024). Questions without Answers: Enjoyment of Irresolution in Mystery Player Experience. Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction 8(CHI PLAY), 1-28. doi:10.1145/3677100. abstract. Analysis of five mystery games by a player-researcher; concludes irresolution can be enjoyable. Not a measurement of players, and it excludes detective mysteries with clear answers.
- **Rating.** no evidence.
- **Inference.** Let the player's solution count and test what happens when a player gets there first. Don't rely on either "they love being ahead" or "they must never be ahead".

# 4. Viewpoint

## M25. First-person narration does not reliably raise identification or transportation

- **Claim.** One influential study found first-person narration plus an in-group character gave the most experience-taking. Later experiments frequently found no effect of first vs third person on identification, transportation or empathy, and one found it raises social presence instead.
- **Citations.**
  - Kaufman, Libby (2012). Changing beliefs and behavior through experience-taking. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 103(1), 1-19. doi:10.1037/a0027525. abstract. Six studies. Study 4: first-person in-group version gave highest experience-taking and most behaviour change.
  - Christy (2017). I, You, or He: Examining the Impact of Point of View on Narrative Persuasion. Media Psychology 21(4), 700-718. doi:10.1080/15213269.2017.1400443. abstract. First, second and third person: no effect on transportation or identification; third person gave more positive attitudes toward gay people.
  - Krause, Weber (2018). Lift Me Up by Looking Down: Social Comparison Effects of Narratives. Frontiers in Psychology 9. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01889. abstract. Perspective manipulation, N = 79, no effect on transportation or experience-taking.
  - Siegenthaler, Fahr (2023). First-Person Versus Third-Person. European Journal of Health Communication 4(3), 35-52. doi:10.47368/ejhc.2023.303. abstract. Explainer videos, N = 364: no effect on identification or attitudes.
  - Ahrens-Schwabe, Kosch, Boomgaarden, Stocker (2026), see claim 18. abstract. Novella: narrative voice no effect on social emotions.
  - Kim, Kim, Wojcieszak, Igartua, Lim (2019). The Presence of the Protagonist: Explaining Narrative Perspective Effects Through Social Presence. Media Psychology 23(6), 891-914. doi:10.1080/15213269.2019.1665548. abstract. N = 503 and 410. First person raised social presence of the protagonist, not identification.
  - Hoeken, Kolthoff, Sanders (2016). Story Perspective and Character Similarity as Drivers of Identification and Narrative Persuasion. Human Communication Research 42(2), 292-311. doi:10.1111/hcre.12076. abstract. N = 120 x 2. Whose perspective the story takes influences identification, and similarity can block it. Tests whose side, not which pronoun.
  - Li, Wang, Liu (2026). Narrative perspective and immersion in literary reading: Experimental evidence from behavioural and neural measures. Poetics 117, 102110. doi:10.1016/j.poetic.2026.102110. not opened; no abstract retrievable.
- **Replication.** I found no direct replication of Study 4 and no meta-analysis of first vs third person. Note that some of these are persuasion studies whose outcomes differ.
- **Rating.** contested, leaning to no reliable effect on identification. moderate that whose perspective is told matters.
- **Age.** Mostly adults; ages not read in several. Not tested in adolescents here apart from claim 23.
- **Inference.** First person doesn't buy identification by itself. Choose it for voice, secrets and presence. Spend identification effort on similarity and on the character's situation.

## M26. Delaying a character's out-group reveal raised experience-taking, in one paper

- **Claim.** Revealing a character's out-group identity early inhibited experience-taking; revealing it later raised experience-taking, reduced stereotype application, and improved attitudes toward the group.
- **Citation.** Kaufman, Libby (2012), as in claim 25, Studies 5 and 6. abstract. Lowering self-concept accessibility also raised experience-taking (Studies 1 to 3).
- **Replication.** none found.
- **Rating.** weak.
- **Inference.** A reader may take on a character more easily when defining differences come after the bond. Single-paper evidence, and it should not be read as advice to hide identity for effect.

## M27. Camera viewpoint in games: unanswered

- **Status.** The perspective searches returned narration studies. A citation scan of Hefner et al. 2007 for first-person, third-person and camera terms found nothing I could use. No source opened on first vs third-person camera. Budget exhausted before a dedicated search.
- **Rating.** not assessed.

## M28. Silent vs voiced protagonists: no controlled comparison found

- **Status.** One search. Essays and a Wikipedia entry argue both ways; a Drexel master's thesis on silent player characters was visible only by contents and not used. Closest measurement is Hitchens et al. 2012 (claim 13), where story and character information did not change identification while player traits did.
- **Rating.** no evidence.
- **Inference.** Neither the "silence lets you project" nor the "voice makes you care" claim is measured.

## M29. Avatar customisation raises identification and short-term persistence; one trial shows less attrition

- **Claim.** Customising an avatar raised identification, motivation and in one three-week study retention. Identification went with more effort and time played.
- **Citations.**
  - Birk, Atkins, Bowey, Mandryk (2016). Fostering Intrinsic Motivation through Avatar Identification in Digital Games. CHI, 2982-2995. doi:10.1145/2858036.2858062. abstract. N = 126, endless runner; identification related to autonomy, immersion, effort, enjoyment and time played.
  - Birk, Mandryk (2018). Combating Attrition in Digital Self-Improvement Programs using Avatar Customization. CHI, 1-15. doi:10.1145/3173574.3174234. abstract. N = 250, daily one-minute breathing exercise for three weeks; customised avatar gave significantly less attrition and more logins.
  - Birk, Mandryk (2019). Improving the Efficacy of Cognitive Training for Digital Mental Health Interventions Through Avatar Customization: Crowdsourced Quasi-Experimental Study. Journal of Medical Internet Research 21(1), e10133. doi:10.2196/10133. abstract. N = 317. Interaction on anxiety ηp2 = .027.
  - Koulouris, Jeffery, Best, O'Neill, Lutteroth (2020). Me vs. Super(wo)man: Effects of Customization and Identification in a VR Exergame. CHI, 1-17. doi:10.1145/3313831.3376661. abstract. Customising raised identification, motivation and performance; idealised avatars raised wishful identification but lowered performance.
  - Trepte, Reinecke (2010). Avatar Creation and Video Game Enjoyment. Journal of Media Psychology 22(4), 171-184. doi:10.1027/1864-1105/a000022. abstract. Quasi-experimental, N = 666; identification strongly related to enjoyment; with identification controlled, similarity related negatively to enjoyment, read as identity play.
  - Rahill, Sebrechts (2021). Effects of Avatar player-similarity and player-construction on gaming performance. Computers in Human Behavior Reports 4, 100131. doi:10.1016/j.chbr.2021.100131. abstract. Similarity and player construction both raised performance and presence.
  - Contrary: Wauck, Lucas, Shapiro, Feng, Boberg, Gratch (2018). Analyzing the Effect of Avatar Self-Similarity on Men and Women in a Search and Rescue Game. CHI, 1-12. doi:10.1145/3173574.3174059. abstract. Photorealistic self vs friend: no effect on performance or experience.
- **Replication.** Direction repeats across labs. The attrition result is one study, by the authors of the method, in a non-game exercise. No meta-analysis found.
- **Rating.** moderate for identification and motivation. weak for retention. no evidence in a story game with a fixed voiced protagonist.
- **Age.** Crowd workers and students; adults mostly.
- **Inference.** This is the one design move here with measured retention behind it, and it is in tension with a fixed, voiced first-person lead. Options to test: cosmetic or in-world customisation that leaves the voice alone. Whether that keeps the effect is unknown.

# 5. Cast

## M30. Stereotyped programmer imagery lowers women's interest in computing; a role model's stereotypicality matters more than gender

- **Claim.** Changing objects in a computing room from stereotypical to neutral raised women undergraduates' interest to men's level. A two-minute meeting with a stereotypical computing role model lowered women's interest immediately and two weeks later, while the role model's gender had no effect.
- **Citations.**
  - Cheryan, Plaut, Davies, Steele (2009). Ambient belonging: How stereotypical cues impact gender participation in computer science. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 97(6), 1045-1060. doi:10.1037/a0016239. abstract. Four studies; men unaffected.
  - Cheryan, Drury, Vichayapai (2013). Enduring Influence of Stereotypical Computer Science Role Models on Women's Academic Aspirations. Psychology of Women Quarterly 37(1), 72-79. doi:10.1177/0361684312459328. full (results). N = 100 undergraduate women, not CS majors. d = .52 at once and d = .46 at two weeks. Mean interest low in all cells (about 1.1 to 1.6). Role model gender F < 1. Belonging mediated.
  - Rosenberger, Claypool (2023). The impact of gender-exclusive language on women's anticipated ostracism: A preregistered replication of Stout and Dasgupta (2011). PLOS ONE 18(9), e0290709. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0290709. abstract. High-powered, preregistered, full replication on job-description language.
- **Limits.** Undergraduates and interest in a major, not a game. Interest was measured in non-CS women; effect on someone who chose to learn programming is unknown.
- **Replication.** No direct replication of the Cheryan studies found by me (search budget). The language result has a preregistered replication.
- **Rating.** moderate for computing interest. no evidence inside games.
- **Age.** Undergraduates only here.
- **Inference.** For a story that teaches or celebrates a technical skill, the expert characters' imagery may matter more than their gender count. Avoid the lone hacker aesthetic in those who stand for that skill, and don't assume adding a female coder fixes it if she embodies the same stereotype.

## M31. A sexist instructor reduces women's learning in VR; avatar gender doesn't

- **Claim.** Female college students learned less from a sexist-behaving male instructor in a virtual classroom. Avatar gender made no difference.
- **Citation.** Chang, Luo, Walton, Aguilar, Bailenson (2019). Stereotype Threat in Virtual Learning Environments: Effects of Avatar Gender and Sexist Behavior on Women's Math Learning Outcomes. Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking 22(10), 634-640. doi:10.1089/cyber.2019.0106.
- **Opened.** abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** 2 x 2, N = 76 women. No effect size in the abstract.
- **Replication.** none seen. Stereotype threat itself has a contested replication record, which I did not review (see Unanswered).
- **Rating.** weak.
- **Inference.** Characters who teach carry risk that characters who merely appear don't. A condescending mentor written for colour should be checked against learning outcomes.

## M32. Sexualised game content and player attitudes: small or null on average, disputed

- **Claim.** A meta-analysis found sexualisation in games not significantly related to well-being or body dissatisfaction, nor to sexism. A three-year panel found no relation between game use and sexist attitudes. Other correlational work reports associations with STEM sexism, and one longitudinal study shows that representation alone did not reduce sexism.
- **Citations.**
  - Ferguson, Sauer, Drummond, Kneer, Lowe-Calverley (2022). Does sexualization in video games cause harm in players? A meta-analytic examination. Computers in Human Behavior 135, 107341. doi:10.1016/j.chb.2022.107341. abstract. Well-being r = 0.082, k = 10, n = 2,010, p = .066. Sexism r = 0.040, k = 15, n = 15,938, p = .070. Better designed studies found less. Lead author is a known sceptic of media effects; my characterisation.
  - Breuer, Kowert, Festl, Quandt (2015). Sexist Games=Sexist Gamers? A Longitudinal Study on the Relationship Between Video Game Use and Sexist Attitudes. Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking 18(4), 197-202. doi:10.1089/cyber.2014.0492. abstract. Representative German players 14 and older, N = 824, three years. No relation. It measures amount and genre, not content directly.
  - Ferguson, Donnellan (2017). Are Associations Between "Sexist" Video Games and Decreased Empathy Toward Women Robust? A Reanalysis of Gabbiadini et al. 2016. Journal of Youth and Adolescence 46(12), 2446-2459. doi:10.1007/s10964-017-0700-x. abstract. Reanalysis of 153 adolescents, mean age 16.8. Little evidence of overall effect, and results depended on modelling.
  - Contrary: Ellithorpe, Yao, Rhodes (2025). Lifetime Video Game Exposure is Associated with Internalization of Sexist STEM Beliefs in Women and Men. Media Psychology 29(2), 171-194. doi:10.1080/15213269.2025.2475047. abstract. N = 1,762 undergraduates and 653 adults. Correlational; stronger for women.
  - Lemmens, Kühne (2026). Reducing sexism through Representation: A longitudinal study on the impact of female footballers in FC24 on hostile sexism. Entertainment Computing 58, 101156. doi:10.1016/j.entcom.2026.101156. abstract. 486 players, three waves. Consistent aversion to playable women among the mostly male base; more play later linked to more hostility; representation alone was not enough. Set in the game's most popular online mode. Early in the waves more play went with slightly less sexism; this reversed later.
- **Replication.** Disputed between labs; the meta-analysis does not end it.
- **Rating.** moderate that average measured effect on attitudes is small. contested on interpretation. All are harm outcomes; none measures wanting to keep playing.
- **Age.** Breuer spans 14 and up. Ferguson and Donnellan are adolescents. Ellithorpe is adults.
- **Inference.** The evidence neither licenses sexualised casting as harmless nor shows it damages attitudes. The decision rests on who it drives away and how it fits the story, and claim 33 covers what little is known on that.

## M33. Balanced casts and audience interest: associations only, no controlled test found

- **Claim.** Industry reports associate diverse casts with higher box office. No experiment manipulating cast balance and measuring audience interest was found. Depiction of men and women on game covers had no sales association, while sexualised covers had a negative association in major genres.
- **Citations.**
  - Valentowitsch (2024). Does sex sell? Gender representation, sexualization, and violence on video game covers and their impact on sales. Journal of Business Strategies 41(1), 27-42. doi:10.54155/jbs.41.1.27-42. abstract. 1,113 games. Male and female depiction and violence: no statistical impact. Sexualised depictions: negative in key genres and segments, positive in smaller ones such as puzzle and simulation. Observational, small journal.
  - CAA (2017), reported by Slate (https://www.slate.com/blogs/browbeat/2017/06/21/new_caa_study_movies_with_diverse_casts_make_more_money.html) and NBC. not opened; search summary only: 413 films, at least 30% non-white cast opened better. An agency report, not peer reviewed.
  - UCLA Hollywood Diversity Report, reported at shootonline.com and nofilmschool.com. not opened; search summary only: 31 to 40% cast of colour highest median among top films, later a "sweet spot". Correlational and advocacy-linked.
  - Lemmens, Kühne (2026), claim 32: community aversion inside one game's competitive mode.
- **Searched.** One search on cast diversity, Bechdel returns and consumer discrimination; results were press and industry reports. Citation scans of the reference lists of Rubin, Beyond the box office (2022, 10.1016/j.jbusres.2022.07.021, title filter only) and Valentowitsch 2024 found no experiment.
- **Rating.** weak for association. no evidence for a causal effect either way.
- **Inference.** Neither "balanced casts raise interest" nor "balanced casts lower it" is measured for story games. Decide on other grounds; don't cite the box office figures as proof.

# 6. Dark and adult content

Two cases are kept apart, as the evidence differs: graphic violence or sex as content, and emotional darkness such as loss.

## M34. Violence draws selection but on average doesn't raise enjoyment

- **Claim.** A meta-analysis found violence raises choosing but lowers enjoyment. Experiments removing or adding violence repeatedly found it didn't help enjoyment. In games, violent content added little to enjoyment or motivation once competence and autonomy were considered. One later meta-analysis reports d = 0.33 on enjoyment in an unclear direction.
- **Citations.**
  - Weaver (2011). A Meta-Analytical Review of Selective Exposure to and the Enjoyment of Media Violence. Journal of Broadcasting & Electronic Media 55(2), 232-250. doi:10.1080/08838151.2011.570826. abstract, no numbers given.
  - Weaver, Kobach (2012). The Relationship Between Selective Exposure and the Enjoyment of Television Violence. Aggressive Behavior 38(2), 175-184. doi:10.1002/ab.21417. abstract. N = 191. Chose violent descriptions; enjoyed nonviolent versions more, even if they had chosen the violent description.
  - Weaver, Wilson (2009). The Role of Graphic and Sanitized Violence in the Enjoyment of Television Dramas. Human Communication Research 35(3), 442-463. doi:10.1111/j.1468-2958.2009.01358.x. abstract. Over 400 participants, five programmes edited three ways. Nonviolent most enjoyable after controlling for perceived action, regardless of sex, aggression or sensation seeking.
  - Sparks, Sherry, Lubsen (2005). The Appeal of Media Violence in a Full-length Motion Picture: An Experimental Investigation. Communication Reports 18(1-2), 21-30. doi:10.1080/08934210500084198. abstract. College students, N = 134, The Fugitive with almost all violence deleted: no change in enjoyment or quality.
  - Przybylski, Ryan, Rigby (2009). The Motivating Role of Violence in Video Games. Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 35(2), 243-259. doi:10.1177/0146167208327216. abstract. Six studies. Enjoyment, value and desire for future play went with autonomy and competence; violence added little unique variance. High trait aggression players preferred violent games though it did not reliably enhance their enjoyment.
  - Hoffner, Levine (2005). Enjoyment of Mediated Fright and Violence: A Meta-Analysis. Media Psychology 7(2), 207-237. doi:10.1207/s1532785xmep0702_5. abstract. Enjoyment higher in men, those lower in empathy, and those higher in sensation seeking and aggressiveness.
  - Xie, Lee (2008). Anticipated Violence, Arousal, and Enjoyment of Movies: Viewers' Reactions to Violent Previews Based on Arousal-Seeking Tendency. The Journal of Social Psychology 148(3), 277-292. doi:10.3200/socp.148.3.277-292. abstract. 159 college students. High arousal seekers anticipated more enjoyment after violent previews.
  - Contrary or unclear: Rasul, Shin (2021). Morality, Technology, and Enjoyment: Meta Analyzing the Enjoyment of Mediated Violence. Atlantic Journal of Communication 30(4), 337-353. doi:10.1080/15456870.2021.1927035. abstract. Fixed-effect d = 0.33. The abstract doesn't state the direction; video-game and morality moderators heterogeneous.
- **Replication.** Experiments agree in direction; Rasul and Shin is unresolved. Weaver and Wilson's result depends on controlling perceived action, so violent versions may gain through action, which is a real difference.
- **Rating.** moderate that violence as such doesn't raise average enjoyment. moderate that it raises selection. contested at the margin by Rasul and Shin and by individual differences.
- **Age.** Mostly college students and adults; good fit for adults. Youth findings not reviewed.
- **Inference.** Violence may help a marketing blurb and not the story people finish. A dark premise that gets the click isn't evidence it holds them. If a scene has violence, keep it for action or consequence. Audiences differ, so a horror-seeking group may behave differently.

## M35. Sexual content: doesn't help ad outcomes; its effect on story enjoyment isn't measured well

- **Claim.** Sex in media or ads doesn't help ad memory or purchase. In movie previews, sex and violence were not directly associated with higher anticipated enjoyment, only indirectly via suspense or humour. I found no experiment on sexual content raising enjoyment in a story game.
- **Citations.**
  - Lull, Bushman (2015). Do sex and violence sell? A meta-analytic review of the effects of sexual and violent media and ad content on memory, attitudes, and buying intentions. Psychological Bulletin 141(5), 1022-1048. doi:10.1037/bul0000018. abstract. 53 experiments, 8,489 participants. Violent media hurt brand outcomes; sexual ads lowered brand attitudes; sexual media no significant effect on memory or intention; congruence helped.
  - Oliver, Kalyanaraman, Mahood, Ramasubramanian (2007). Sexual and Violent Imagery in Movie Previews: Effects on Viewers' Perceptions and Anticipated Enjoyment. Journal of Broadcasting & Electronic Media 51(4), 596-614. doi:10.1080/08838150701626446. abstract. Two experiments.
  - Valentowitsch (2024), claim 33.
- **Limits.** Lull and Bushman measure advertising outcomes, not whether the programme was enjoyed; this is widely misread. Anticipated enjoyment isn't enjoyment.
- **Replication.** Meta-analysis, heterogeneous.
- **Rating.** moderate for ads. weak for trailers and covers. no evidence for story enjoyment in games.
- **Age.** Adults and students.
- **Inference.** The "sex sells" justification has no support for keeping players in a story. A romance or sexual storyline must be justified by other evidence or tested.

## M36. Emotional darkness: sad and uncomfortable moments can be valued

- **Claim.** Players described negative emotions in games as enjoyed and appreciated, with character attachment, loss and lack of agency as causes. Story goes with appreciation more than enjoyment.
- **Citations.**
  - Bopp, Mekler, Opwis (2016). Negative Emotion, Positive Experience? CHI, 2996-3006. doi:10.1145/2858036.2858227. abstract. 121 players recalling a moving game experience. Self-selected memorable cases; no comparison group.
  - Oliver et al. (2016), claim 13. N = 512, recall.
  - Daneels, Malliet, Geerts, Denayer, Walrave, Vandebosch (2021). Assassins, Gods, and Androids: How Narratives and Game Mechanics Shape Eudaimonic Game Experiences. Media and Communication 9(1), 49-61. doi:10.17645/mac.v9i1.3205. abstract. Qualitative game analysis of three games; stronger moving experiences near the end. A researcher's analysis, not players.
  - Cole, Gillies (2019). Thinking and Doing: Challenge, Agency, and the Eudaimonic Experience in Video Games. Games and Culture 16(2), 187-207. doi:10.1177/1555412019881536. abstract. Interviews; framework of four kinds of agency.
- **Replication.** Direction consistent, method retrospective.
- **Rating.** moderate that such moments are valued when remembered. no evidence that they raise continuation. Those who quit at a bleak point aren't in the samples.
- **Age.** Ages not read.
- **Inference.** Treat emotional stakes and graphic content as separate decisions: this claim gives some support to the first and claim 34 gives none to the second. Survivorship is a real risk: measure stops around the bleak scene.

# 7. Story alongside learning (only what the library's files lack)

## M37. A story-making programming environment raised voluntary time without changing learning (girls aged about 12)

- **Claim.** Girls using a storytelling version of Alice learned basic programming as well as those using the generic version, found it equally easy and entertaining, and spent 42% more time programming; 51% vs 16% snuck extra time.
- **Citation.** Kelleher, Pausch, Kiesler (2007). Storytelling Alice motivates middle school girls to learn computer programming. CHI, 1455-1464. doi:10.1145/1240624.1240844.
- **Opened.** full (cited sections); the 51% and 16% figures came from a search summary of the paper, the 42% and "more than 3 times" from the abstract.
- **Design, sample, effect.** 88 girls, mean age 12.6, nearly all grades 5 to 9, about 2 h 15 min. The learner makes the story, which differs from a game telling one. Developer-run.
- **Replication.** none found by me.
- **Rating.** moderate for that age and setting. weak for adults.
- **Age.** Children and early teens only.
- **Inference.** Fits the library's earlier conclusion (B3 in EVIDENCE-CODE-GAMES-AI.md) that story moves engagement rather than learning, with one more data point on real extra time. Note it's about authoring story; whether reading or watching one does the same is untested here.

## M38. Surprise in a learning game can help deeper understanding, with weak support

- **Claim.** Surprising events tied to the material left engagement and surface learning unchanged but improved knowledge structure in one game; in another they showed no overall effect at first, then a marginal one in a follow-up limited to higher-level students.
- **Citations.**
  - van der Spek, van Oostendorp, Meyer (2012). Introducing surprising events can stimulate deep learning in a serious game. British Journal of Educational Technology 44(1), 156-169. doi:10.1111/j.1467-8535.2011.01282.x. abstract. N = 41, triage training.
  - Wouters, van Oostendorp, ter Vrugte, vanderCruysse, de Jong, Elen (2016). The effect of surprising events in a serious game on learning mathematics. British Journal of Educational Technology 48(3), 860-877. doi:10.1111/bjet.12458. abstract. Two experiments, prevocational students; a character appears and changes the problem. No overall difference in the first, with a hint of benefit for higher-level students. The second used higher-level students: a marginal overall effect, stronger once prior skill was included.
- **Replication.** Same research group.
- **Rating.** weak.
- **Age.** Wouters is school-age; van der Spek age not read.
- **Inference.** A plot surprise that changes the problem may be doing teaching work and isn't only decoration. Weak evidence, and it needs a delayed test. It may not raise engagement.

## M39. Narrative vs expository text for learning: not reached

- **Status.** Unanswered. The one combined search returned only the surprise work, and the budget ran out. Claims 9, 12 and 18 also bear on learning.
- **Rating.** not assessed.

# Method and honesty notes

**Search budget.** 25 web search calls. The tool ran extra sub-searches inside three compound queries, which I counted, giving 30 of 30. Budget ran out before a dedicated search for stereotype threat in games, game camera perspective, and narrative vs expository memory.

**Other lookups, not counted as searches.** Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar and Europe PMC by exact DOI, and citation lists on an exact DOI ("citation scan": title filter over works citing or cited by a paper already found). These found many sources here, and that route isn't a full literature search; it favours what is already connected. One Crossref bibliographic lookup was used to find Hoeken's DOI after my guess failed. One OpenAlex keyword query was run by mistake; its result was not used.

**Where sources came from.** Sources were named by a search, a repository page or an opened paper's citation lists. Exceptions and details: DOIs of some works named by a search were typed from memory and then confirmed against Crossref. Reagan 2016's DOI came from my recollection rather than a search result; I then read its record and used it for a citation scan.

**DOI checking.** A script checked each DOI against Crossref for year and first author and reports misses. It also ran negative controls. The result is the last section of this file.

**What checking caught.**
- My guessed DOI for Cheryan's role-model paper resolved to a different Cheryan paper. Corrected to doi:10.1177/0361684312459328.
- My guessed Hoeken DOI did not resolve; the Crossref record has a 1999 PII although published in 2000.
- A repository page spells Hitchens as "Hichens"; Crossref's spelling is used.
- A search summary called Elson 2014 a study; the abstract shows a theoretical argument, so it's not cited as data.
- Fendt's title suggests choice can be faked. Its own second hypothesis failed, which is what claim 17 reports.
- Lin's thesis and CAA and UCLA reports could not be opened, and are marked so.

**Inferences** are mine and untested unless stated. Ratings were set conservatively after the note that an earlier pass rated too high: a single paper from one group is at most moderate, whatever its N.

**Corrections from checking my own draft.** I checked a sample of the draft's specific statements against the downloaded text, abstracts and the session log (not every number). That removed or rewrote: a description of Hoeken and van Vliet's stimulus with no source behind it; a claim that a search summary misdescribed Fendt, which I couldn't find; a mention of Daneels's "adolescent work", also unsourced; a heading on claim 39 asserting a result no study here supports; and a heading and "strong" rating on claim 15 saying players stop early, which the abstracts I read don't show. Statements not in that sample rest on my notes from reading.
