# Story craft evidence: what practitioners say and show

**Spoiler warning, before any work is named.** This file discusses the plot or structure of The Murder of Roger Ackroyd, Lost, BioShock, Spec Ops: The Line, Firewatch, Her Story, Return of the Obra Dinn, The Case of the Golden Idol, Ace Attorney, Disco Elysium, 80 Days, Half-Life and Half-Life 2, Portal 2, Dragon Age, Baldur's Gate 3, Heaven's Vault, Uncharted, Fallout 3, Detroit: Become Human and Glass. Specific plot reveals are held in the last section, "Reveals", tagged by work and by where in the work the source places them; stop before that heading to avoid them. The text above it still says, at a general level, that a work has a late turn, keeps one ending or implicates its player, and it describes openings and premises. Where a work's trick is itself the reveal, the claim doesn't name the work.

Date of research: 2026-11-06. Budget: 30 of 30 web searches (28 calls; two returned two searches each, counted as two). No rules or skill text here. Measured research is in EVIDENCE-MEASURED.md (`[M8]`); the measured items below are only those that bear directly on craft or on how generated fiction fails.

## Most likely to change how a story is written

All inference from the claims cited. None of this has been tested on a game.

1. **Measured AI failure is sameness, and a score the authors read as pacing, not style.** LLM stories are read as accelerating and leaving suspense unresolved; plots echo across runs and models; 88% of stories in one sample use one of 11 words, and temperature is reported to do little (73, 74, 75). Plan twists, foreshadowing and names outside the model. Banned-name lists go stale per model (76).
2. **Separate what must be reached from what must be reasoned.** Plot-critical conclusions need several routes (19). A clue that skips the puzzle is a bug when the puzzle is the point (24). They look contradictory and are two cases.
3. **Stalls should have planned story events**, and being stuck on a technicality after understanding the mystery counts as failure (20, 24).
4. **A fixed plot is defensible; broken promises aren't.** Ingold: "You don't" make choices matter (31). Short places Firewatch's failure in midgame promises, not its fixed ending (40). Hocking shows the danger of blaming the player for a forced action (37).
5. **Make output a fact in the plot.** Contradiction exposed by action is Ace Attorney's engine; Obra Dinn and Her Story make the interface the investigation (23, 25, 26, 39).
6. **Fair play reduces to a few tests:** indicated before, explains everything, surprises, author doesn't lie, world rules given (14, 15, 50). Many listed rules are period pieces. A first-person narrator may conceal if every line is true on re-reading (17).
7. **Adult content is a depiction and frequency decision.** Frequent strong language points to M and R-equivalent regardless of nudity (59 to 62a). Profanity needs a pass and good acting (69). Violence is judged cheap when claimed meaningful with no consequence (68, 70).
8. **Romance is a story choice, and you can't serve both camps** (Gaider, 63). Content revealing a character shouldn't sit behind romance (64). Nobody describes a good decline path; Firewatch's written silence is closest (66).

## How to read this

**Read levels.**
- `raw`: I read the page text or PDF text myself, not always the whole page. Quotes were checked against it.
- `extract`: opened through a fetch tool that returns a small model's summary of the page, not the page. The attributions and short quotes are as the tool reported them, unchecked. It is fallible: in one result it named a "Kim Faliszek" nobody had asked about or written. Check any `extract` quote before using it verbatim.
- `not opened`: known only from a search-result summary or another source's citation. This is second-hand even when the wording sounds direct.

**Kinds.**
- `practitioner`: a named maker says it about their craft.
- `critic`: a scholar, reviewer or blogger says it. Not a maker of the work discussed.
- `demonstrated`: a game or show demonstrably does it. Says nothing on whether it worked unless stated.
- `measured`: a study with a sample.
- `official`: the text of a rating body.
- `inference`: mine.

**No script checked these sources.** Unlike EVIDENCE-MEASURED.md, this file reports no machine check of its addresses; the read level above is the check.

**Ratings are about the source, not the truth of the advice.** Standing: high (lead maker of a shipped, well-regarded work, or a recognised scholar), medium, low (student, hobbyist, anonymous, wiki). Corroboration: wide, some, single, contested. Craft advice from a high-standing source is still not evidence that the advice works. Almost nothing in Q1 to Q7 was tested.

**Same-name traps.** Ken Levine in this file is the TV writer (MASH, Cheers), not the BioShock director. Marc Laidlaw wrote Half-Life; Mike Laidlaw was creative director of Dragon Age. Leigh Alexander (journalist) and Justin Alexander (RPG designer) are different people.

# Q1. Structure

**C1. Three-act structure as commonly taught is a timing template that Syd Field popularised in 1979: 30/60/30 minutes of a 120-minute film, with plot points ending acts one and two. Field presented it as an analysis of hits.**
- Who: David Bordwell summarising Field. https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/?p=27870
- Read: extract. Field's book not opened.
- Kind: critic, reporting a practitioner.
- Standing and corroboration: high; wide (Screenplayology agrees, claim 3).
- Limits: a film feature template. Nothing about games, serials or stills.
- Would change (inference): treat page and minute positions as film-specific. Use "where do the turns fall" as a question, not a target.

**C2. Evidence that studio-era writers used acts is thin. Steven Price found little direct evidence for the 1930s and 40s; Claus Tieber found no discussion of it in studio records; Bordwell found only scattered references. Fitzgerald's 1938 Infidelity timetable (acts of 45, 50 and 25 pages, built from sequences) is his one clear case. The three-part idea appears in manuals from at least 1913 and rests on a misreading of Aristotle.**
- Who: Bordwell, citing Price, Tieber, Fitzgerald via Latham. Same URL.
- Read: extract. Price and Tieber not opened.
- Kind: critic (archival).
- Standing and corroboration: high; some.
- Limits: Bordwell calls for more evidence and printed a reader's correction on one claim, and replied that the passages led him to his view: that readers discard scripts for missing page-number turns. He says he never supervised analysts.
- Would change (inference): don't defend a structure as "how it has always been done". Fitzgerald shows sequences inside acts in 1938, so the two systems aren't rivals.

**C3. Rival counts disagree. Kristin Thompson finds four large parts (set-up, complicating action, development, climax, plus epilogue) from studying 10 Hollywood films, and calls Field's second act out of proportion. McKee endorses three as a minimum and describes four, five and eight. James Bonnet says three-act is not a story structure at all.**
- Who: Screenplayology (Andrew Kenneth Gay credited) https://www.screenplayology.com/?p=805 ; Bordwell on Thompson.
- Read: extract. Thompson, McKee, Bonnet books not opened.
- Kind: critic reporting critics and practitioners.
- Standing and corroboration: high for Thompson; contested by design.
- Limits: 10 films is a small sample; Thompson's model is descriptive.
- Would change (inference): for a long middle, plan by goal changes every quarter or so, not one "act two". This matches Thompson's reasoning that parts turn on goals and new information.

**C4. The one quantitative test of plot-point positions I read found all seven in the top 10 IMDb films, close to theory: inciting incident mean 10.2% of runtime, act one break 23.9, midpoint 50.3 (SD 1.4), low point 69.9, act two break 75.1, climax 90.6, resolution 98.5. The author concludes these are "structural landmarks", more fixed at the midpoint and climax than the start.**
- Who: Eoin O'Brien, RSU International Research Conference proceedings, 2026. https://rsucon.rsu.ac.th/files/proceedings/RSUSOC2026/IN26-059.pdf
- Read: raw.
- Kind: measured, weakly.
- Standing and corroboration: low to medium (conference proceedings, single author); he cites Cutting 2016 and Papalampidi et al. 2019 as supporting, neither opened.
- Limits, stated: single analyst, no inter-rater check. Limits, mine (inference): the coder knew the expected positions and fixed definitions beforehand, and picked among plausible scenes, so finding them near the target is partly built in. Ten films, no low-rated comparison, so it can't link structure to quality. It describes films that are successful and says nothing on cause.
- Would change (inference): at most, a midpoint that changes the question is a reasonable default. Not evidence for any system.

**C5. No study I found shows any named structure causes better stories. Evidence "beyond practice" is at best descriptive (claim 4). For Harmon, Save the Cat, Truby, the sequence approach and kishotenketsu I found none at all.**
- Who: my search of this literature (searches 2, 19, 20).
- Kind: inference from a limited search, not proof of absence.
- Would change (inference): rules should call these planning aids, not findings.
- Read: not applicable; a statement about what the searches returned.
- Corroboration: none needed; absence claim.
- Limits: 30 searches can't prove none exists. Cutting 2016 and Papalampidi 2019 weren't opened.

**C6. Harmon's circle: eight steps adapted from Campbell's Hero's Journey, returning to the start with change. Written at Channel 101 around 2003.**
- Who: Harmon, "Story Structure 101". https://channel101.fandom.com/wiki/Story_Structure_101:_Super_Basic_Shit (refused: HTTP 402). Details from secondary sites, e.g. https://storytellingdb.com/the-dan-harmon-story-circle-a-practical-guide-to-storytelling/
- Read: not opened. Harmon's own text was not reached. Wired's profile could not be fetched (tool refused the domain).
- Kind: practitioner, second-hand.
- Standing and corroboration: Harmon high; the explainer sites low.
- Limits: **Harmon's own claims about scope and failure are unanswered.** Everything circulating is content-marketing paraphrase. Inference: reported as an episode tool from a TV writer, so most plausibly suited to a short closed unit that returns to status quo with change.
- Would change (inference): possibly a shape for single episodes. No basis yet for treating it as a season shape.

**C7. Save the Cat: reported as a 15-beat, page-keyed sheet. Peter Suderman (Slate, 2013) argued Hollywood's sameness comes from it and said it forced him to reorganise his own essay. Replies: Christopher Boone says the fault is mechanical page-hitting, not structure; the Bitter Script Reader (ex-reader, TV writer) says the book contains nothing new; Ken Levine (TV writer) accepts many films follow it and warns it makes "paint-by-the-numbers" work, usable as a starting point.**
- Who: https://nofilmschool.com/2013/07/screenwriter-reliance-story-structure-ruined-movies ; https://thebitterscriptreader.blogspot.com/2013/08/why-save-cat-didnt-destroy.html ; https://kenlevine.blogspot.com/2013/08/should-we-save-cat.html
- Read: extract for all three. Slate article not opened (404 at the address tried). Snyder's book not opened.
- Kind: critic and practitioner.
- Standing and corroboration: medium to high; contested.
- Limits: anecdote on all sides. Nobody measured sameness or attributed it. Snyder's own defence not read.
- Would change (inference): if used, beats are a checklist of questions on a finished outline, not page targets. An LLM given a beat sheet will likely fill it literally (see claim 80), so a template may increase the sameness risk most in AI drafting.

**C8. Truby's position (that three-act structure damages writing, in favour of steps tied to character) is widely cited, but I did not read it.**
- Who: John Truby, The Anatomy of Story; the Raindance essay. Addresses tried returned 404. Search 19 returned nothing on him.
- Read: not opened. The parenthetical is from memory and unverified.
- Kind: practitioner, unverified.
- Limits: **unanswered**. Don't use this entry as support.
- Corroboration: none read.
- Would change (inference): nothing until read.

**C9. The sequence approach: Frank Daniel's model of three acts holding eight sequences, documented in Gulino's Screenwriting: The Sequence Approach (2013). Separately, analyst manuals cited by Bordwell report four to eight sequences of 10 to 15 pages.**
- Who: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Daniel ; Bordwell citing Lerch 1999.
- Read: extract. Gulino not opened.
- Kind: critic, second-hand.
- Limits: Wikipedia entry carries a needs-citations notice; no criticism found; **authors' claimed failures unanswered**.
- Would change (inference): a sequence of about 10 to 15 minutes maps loosely onto a chapter or level with its own small question. The mapping is untested.
- Corroboration: single chain (Gulino via Wikipedia), plus Lerch via Bordwell.

**C10. Kishotenketsu originates in Chinese quatrain theory; the "no conflict" gloss is popular and overstated. Works using it can contain conflict; what it lacks is structural dependence on conflict, and a third-part turn that may introduce a new element. Extension to narrative may be 20th century. Miyamoto and Hayashida are reported to use it in Mario level design.**
- Who: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu (citing Kirkpatrick 1997, Eurogamer 2015); commenter DMT at https://languagehat.com/kishotenketsu/
- Read: extract. Scholarly sources and Eurogamer not opened.
- Kind: critic; the Nintendo item is practitioner, second-hand.
- Standing and corroboration: low to medium; some.
- Limits: DMT cites nothing. Most English writing on it is blogs. The Nintendo use is level design and not plot.
- Would change (inference): useful for a single case or chapter built on a recontextualising reveal. Don't cite it as a proof that stories don't need conflict.

**C11. Television: A story is the main plot, B and C are subplots. One writer-focused guide offers a heuristic of about three A beats per act and two B beats. Teasers hook before credits; serial episodes don't close but flow on; hour dramas end acts on turns.**
- Who: search-result summaries of glossaries and study guides: https://filmdaft.com/tv-writing-glossary-essential-writers-room-terms-explained/ ; fiveable. TV Calling's site (https://www.tv-calling.com/tag/structure/page/11/, read as extract) says the A story is the real focus and that serial shows divide stories by character and theme.
- Read: not opened (guides); extract (TV Calling).
- Kind: critic, low standing.
- Limits: **no working showrunner was reached on season arcs, midpoints or act-outs. Search 28 aimed at Gilligan and returned nothing. Partly unanswered.** This is what a study guide says, and it fits commercial-break TV. Streaming and a player-paced game have no breaks.
- Would change (inference): treat as vocabulary only. Episode endings in a player-paced serial need testing, not borrowing.
- Corroboration: wide among guides, none from showrunners.

**C12. Lost: Lindelof says the writers wanted to answer mysteries as they went, and that ABC pushed to delay reveals. Critic Alan Sepinwall argues reliance on unanswered boxes eroded trust. Lindelof's stated regret is about how he handled the finale's reception, not about the mystery structure.**
- Who: https://www.slashfilm.com/778524/lost-was-never-supposed-to-be-such-a-confusing-show-so-what-happened (extract). Abrams's 2007 TED talk and Sepinwall via https://popmatters.com/jj-abrams-lost-mystery-boxes-2495671305.html (not opened). Regret via https://uproxx.com/tv/damon-lindelof-regret-amplify-lost-ending (not opened); qz refused.
- Kind: practitioner and critic.
- Standing and corroboration: high; some.
- Limits: self-justifying retrospective. Broadcast seasons of unknown length were part of the problem.
- Would change (inference): a serial should fix answers before posing questions, and plan regular payoffs. The usual warning, drawn from a messy case.
- Read: as listed per source.

# Q2. Mystery

**C13. Knox (1929) and Van Dine (1928) overlap on a core: the reader gets the clues the detective gets; no supernatural solution; no luck or intuition solving it; the culprit is someone met early and, per OU's account of Knox 1 (extract), not someone whose thoughts the reader has followed; the detective isn't the culprit.**
- Who: Knox's list via https://www.open.edu/openlearn/history-the-arts/agatha-christie-and-the-golden-age-detective-fiction/content-section-5.1 ; Van Dine's text at https://www.staff.u-szeged.hu/~gnovak/vandine.htm
- Read: extract for both.
- Kind: practitioner (period).
- Standing and corroboration: high historically; wide. A commenter on https://theinvisibleevent.com/2019/03/02/the-men-who-explain-miracles-episode-9-2 notes Knox's list traces back to his 1924 novel Sanctions, predating Van Dine (unverified).
- Limits: written as a game code for puzzle novels. OU notes it was taught through interwar how-to courses.
- Would change (inference): this core is the part of the fair-play tradition that a mystery writer should keep; see claim 14.

**C14. Sorting the rules (my reading, not a practitioner's list).**
- Hold as craft: clues shown to the reader (Knox 8, Van Dine 1); no tricks on the reader beyond those the criminal plays (Van Dine 2); solved by reasoning (Van Dine 5, 6; Knox 6); culprit seen early, and not one whose thoughts the reader has followed (Knox 1, Van Dine 10); fair on re-reading (Van Dine 15).
- Hold if restated: "no unexplained world rules": no supernatural (Knox 2, Van Dine 8) means a reveal may only use rules the reader was given. Twins signposted (Knox 10) is the same idea.
- Period pieces: Knox 5 ("no Chinaman"), which OU reads as racist stereotyping; Van Dine 11 on servants (class assumption); 3 no romance; 7 must have a corpse; 9 one detective; 13 no secret societies; 16 no description; 17 no professional criminals; 19 no political motive; 20's list of stale tricks.
- Dead for noir specifically: 17 and 19 exclude most of hardboiled crime.
- Kind: inference. Van Dine's numbers are from the text read. Knox's numbers 1, 5 and 8 are confirmed by what I read; the others are from memory and should be checked.
- Limits: a judgement call. Knox's rule 7 (detective not culprit) and 4 (no undiscovered poison) are arguably still useful but rarely tested.
- Would change: the rule set should cite the "hold" group and not the list.
- Who: me, sorting claims 13, 15 and 16.
- Read: as in those claims.
- Corroboration: claim 15 reaches a similar core independently, from low standing.

**C15. A blog commenter's reduction of the rules to five expectations: the solution is indicated beforehand, explains everything before it, surprises, the author doesn't lie, and natural law applies unless prepared. The same commenter says many rules date badly and that the competitive-game analogy doesn't hold.**
- Who: Scott K. Ratner, comment at https://theinvisibleevent.com/2019/03/02/the-men-who-explain-miracles-episode-9-2
- Read: extract.
- Kind: critic.
- Standing and corroboration: low standing; content matches Van Dine 1, 15, and Knox 8.
- Would change (inference): a usable compact test for a mystery reveal, once its low standing is acknowledged.
- Limits: a blog comment; untested; written for novels.

**C16. The Detection Club oath (1930) asks detectives to "well and truly detect" and bars reliance on divine revelation, feminine intuition, coincidence and acts of God. Knox's rules were guidelines; not all members took them seriously. A commenter calls the oath humorous but deliberate.**
- Who: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detection_Club
- Read: extract.
- Kind: critic, about practitioners.
- Standing and corroboration: low; the oath's citation is a weak archived page. Sayers's authorship uncited there.
- Limits: **Sayers's and Queen's own writing on fair play was not opened; unanswered at source.** Queen's "challenge to the reader" was not reached. Takumi compares himself to Queen (claim 25).
- Would change (inference): the oath's list is a short, usable ban on coincidence and unearned rescue as solutions.

**C17. The best-known break of fair play became the genre's most famous book (the work and the trick are in R17). The 1926 NYT review called it "perfectly fair", and John Goddard's 2018 analysis concludes the cheating charge fails.**
- Who: sources listed in R17, since an address names the work.
- Read: extract. An encyclopaedia page read to 100,000 characters of 108,440; the Sayers defence I was looking for did not appear.
- Kind: demonstrated, plus critic.
- Standing and corroboration: medium; some.
- Limits: a one-off trick by its nature. The same source calls the book among the author's most controversial, and quotes a contemporary saying the reader will feel "fairly, or unfairly, sold up".
- Would change (inference): a narrator can conceal, provided every sentence is literally true on re-reading. Directly relevant to a mystery with a first-person narrator.

**C18. Critic JJ argues Knox's rule 1 created a "democratisation of suspicion": anyone met could be guilty. He also holds that readers now recognise the tropes, so surprises that once worked are obvious, but the equal footing still matters.**
- Who: https://theinvisibleevent.com/2019/11/12/knox-decalogue-the-criminal/
- Read: extract.
- Kind: critic.
- Standing and corroboration: low to medium; single.
- Limits: **suspect pool size: no practitioner number was found.**
- Would change (inference): every named character needs to be a credible suspect at some point, and readers will guess the obvious trope, so the structural surprise can't rest on it alone.

**C19. The Three Clue Rule: for any conclusion the players must reach, include at least three clues, because a clue is a plan, and three give two backups; he introduces this with a joke line, "probably miss the first; ignore the second; and misinterpret the third". A single clue fails at several points: not searching, not examining, failing the check, not concluding. Extended: any required problem needs three solutions.**
- Who: Justin Alexander. https://thealexandrian.net/creations/misc/three-clue-rule.html
- Read: raw.
- Kind: practitioner (tabletop GM and designer).
- Standing and corroboration: medium to high in RPG design; wide in that community, per the forums returned.
- Limits: one GM's experience; the essay admits "three" is partly a joke. It's about tabletop, where a GM can improvise; a scripted game can't.
- Would change (inference): for every required conclusion in a story, list the routes to it. A task the player must carry out has an extra failure mode, the attempt doesn't work, so a plot-critical fact should not depend on one successful attempt.

**C20. Corollaries from the same essay: reward clever approaches (permissive clue-finding); when players stall, make something happen that brings new clues (proactive clues), with a last event that closes the scenario anyway; list "revelations" and tick one only when players actually accept it. Red herrings are overrated: players latch onto a wrong conclusion and resist re-assessing. Alexander calls the last-resort close "usually less than satisfactory", and scopes the red-herring point to designing a scenario for an RPG.**
- Who: Alexander, same URL.
- Read: raw for the red-herring passage; extract for others.
- Kind: practitioner.
- Corroboration: some. Takumi's staged optional hints and Klavins's pacing concern point the same way. Contradicted on red herrings by the genre's tradition.
- Limits: "only with extreme caution", not never. Tabletop.
- Would change (inference): plan the stall events, not only the hints. In a serial mystery, keep a tracker of what the player has concluded and not only seen.

**C21. GUMSHOE: the premise is that investigative games are about interpreting clues rather than finding them, so core clues are automatic when a relevant ability is used. Alexander quotes Esoterrorists as making clue-finding "all but automatic" and says Laws removed the clue-finding step.**
- Who: Laws, via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GUMSHOE_System (extract) and https://thealexandrian.net/creations/misc/three-clue-rule.html (raw); https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/29334/roleplaying-games/thought-of-the-day-gumshoe-approach-to-clues (extract).
- Read: **Laws's own text not opened**; his reasoning here is reported by others.
- Kind: practitioner, second-hand.
- Corroboration: some; it's widely known in RPG design.
- Limits: Alexander's counter: finding is only one failure; GUMSHOE keeps a linear trail and can mechanise the deduction too, losing the feel of weighing a body of evidence. He concedes it fits mystery's structure.
- Would change (inference): the distinction is the useful bit: finding, connecting and concluding are three failure points. Decide which of the three a given player is meant to do.

**C22. Hobby GM Strange Ian argues for far more clues ("Information should be everywhere"), since players won't follow a planned line of reasoning.**
- Who: https://strangeaeons.substack.com/p/the-hundred-clue-rule
- Read: extract. Standing low. Kind: practitioner (hobby).
- Limits: a tabletop horror/adventure context in which investigation is not the main challenge.
- Corroboration: Alexander's rule points the same way, Klavins against.
- Would change (inference): none alone; it marks the far end of the range.

**C23. Obra Dinn confirms fates in sets of three, which limits brute force; Pope said he wanted a logic puzzle with environmental clues and did not want to hide information, since it wasn't a horror game. He called the narrative structure hard because the story can only be told at deaths. A critic argues the rule of three still lets players cycle guesses, and that multiple-choice lists leak.**
- Who: Pope via https://gamedeveloper.com/design/for-lucas-pope-i-return-of-the-obra-dinn-i-was-a-bunch-of-appealing-design-problems ; critic Antony de Fault, https://filmstories.co.uk/?p=83249
- Read: extract. GDC talk not opened.
- Kind: demonstrated, practitioner, critic.
- Standing and corroboration: high for Pope; single on the exploit. The check rule itself is visible in play.
- Limits: a solo-authored game with a tight form.
- Would change (inference): an answer check that confirms in batches is a known way to test reasoning. Accept that some players will brute it, and that a menu leaks.

**C24. Golden Idol: a clue that let players bypass many of the steps had to be removed. Testing was playtest interviews, not "hard quantitative data". Non-linear routes were welcome but not required. Pacing mattered: a player who understands the main mystery and is then "stuck for thirty minutes on some technicality, that's bad". Testers found scenarios harder because they played one at a time and forgot earlier context. An unrecognisable apple got redrawn.**
- Who: Andrejs Klavins. https://gamedeveloper.com/business/-the-case-of-the-golden-idol-i-used-frequent-testing-to-improve-its-mystery-solving
- Read: raw.
- Kind: practitioner.
- Standing and corroboration: high; single on clue removal, directly opposed in form to claim 19 (see Disagreements).
- Limits: the earlier "Thought Path" interview was not reached. Small repeat tester group.
- Would change (inference): serial play forgets. Recap and in-world notes matter. And a clue that skips the puzzle is a bug when the puzzle is the point.

**C25. Ace Attorney: Takumi built the game on finding contradictions in testimony and pressing them with evidence, wanting the essence of mystery and not just its mood. Fun comes from each deduction and the reaction, so individual "points" matter most; Queen's logic runs in "lines". Courtroom contradiction produces turnabouts automatically; classic mysteries' middles are "a bit boring". He tests puzzles on staff; a puzzle is too hard when players stay stuck long; hints are optional.**
- Who: Shu Takumi. https://gyakutensaibanlibrary.blogspot.com/2020/12/creator-interview-takumi-shu-2012.html (fan translation of Hayakawa's Mystery Magazine, 2012); https://www.engadget.com/2010-07-02-interview-ghost-trick-and-ace-attorney-director-shu-takumi.html ; https://www.siliconera.com/the-story-of-how-phoenix-wright-ace-attorney-was-born/ (not opened).
- Read: extract. Translation by fans.
- Kind: practitioner, demonstrated.
- Standing and corroboration: high; some.
- Limits: translation wording. Ace Attorney is notoriously linear; players can reach the right answer and still fail the game's chosen step (my knowledge, unsourced here).
- Would change (inference): build scenes around a stated contradiction that an action exposes. A result the player produces that contradicts testimony is a direct fit: play and plot as one action.

**C26. Her Story: Barlow's first instinct was a flowchart; he dropped it. He layered the clips so the story still satisfied in any discovery order, used number-crunching on how connected clips were and how often words recurred, then tested with players. # Q9. How generated fiction fails

Partial overlap with EVIDENCE-MEASURED.md. Items here were read as stated. Vendor interest: Xu et al. (Microsoft), Mirowski et al. (DeepMind), Brzozowski & Chung (Samsung AI Center); Anthropic models appear as subjects in two.

**C73. GPT-4 and LLaMA-3 story continuations repeat plot elements across runs and across models; human plots are rarely re-created. In a Kafka example, 50 of 100 GPT-4 continuations had the policeman say to take the second left. Changing the instruction prompt moved scores little (SD 1.1), and the authors cite work finding temperature's effect on creativity weak.**
- Who: Xu, Jojic, Rao, Brockett, Dolan. PNAS 122(35), 2025; https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.00273
- Read: raw for abstract, method and key results; limits section in the PDF was unreadable in extraction.
- Kind: measured. 100 stories (50 Wikipedia summaries, 50 WritingPrompts), 20 alternatives each.
- Corroboration: some (claims 74, 75, 77); contrary claim 83.
- Limits: 2023-24 models; repetition measured using an LLM's judgement (validated at 0.85 Spearman with humans).

**C74. In the same study, LLM stories "often rapidly accelerate over time without fully resolving the plot": drop ratio 7.8 to 11.3% versus 0.4 to 3.7% for humans. - Limits: short stories; correlation is moderate to weak. Table layout required me to align figures by reading, so take column assignment as stated in the paper's prose ("7 to 9 higher"), not per-cell.

- Corroboration: pacing finding draws on a cited expert-writer study (their ref 7), not opened.

# Where practitioners disagree

**CD1. More clues or fewer.** Alexander: three-plus, no exceptions (19); Strange Ian: everywhere (22); Laws: core clues automatic (21). Klavins: remove any clue that bypasses the steps (24). Inference: these are different cases. A conclusion the plot needs must be easy to reach; a conclusion that is the puzzle must not be skipped. One rule covering both is wrong.
**CD2. Finding versus reasoning.** Laws: finding isn't the fun. Alexander: GUMSHOE's fix addresses only one failure and loses weighing evidence. Takumi and Pope place the fun in reasoning at a point. None tested this.
**CD3. Red herrings.** Alexander says overrated and hazardous; Short says Firewatch's feint harmed it; the Golden Age tradition and Van Dine's rule 2 allow misdirection by the criminal.
**CD4. Fair play as game or not.** Knox and Van Dine: sport with rules. Chandler: contrivance, wants human motive. Ratner: game analogy fails, but expectations hold. The novel of claim 17: break of the rule, later ruled fair by critics.
**CD5. Showing the effect of choices.** Ingold: hide it; visible variables expose the designer. Tavern Crawler and Telltale display it. Taste, mostly.
**CD6. How much branching.** Ingold: far less than it looks. Kurvitz: goes deep, and calls others timid. Gaider: quality over quantity in romance. Budget likely decides.
**CD7. Ludonarrative dissonance.** Hocking: it can break a game. Druckmann: doesn't buy into it. Williams: make it the point. Paez: empty term. Note different tones.
**CD8. Is structure the trouble.** Suderman: templates cause sameness. Boone: mechanical use does. Bitter Script Reader: nothing new there at all. Levine: use, then break. Thompson and Bonnet: the model itself is wrong. Bordwell: the history is overstated. O'Brien: top films match it (weak design).
**CD9. Silence.** Laidlaw: not recommended, yet committed. Critics split between "man of action" and "crutch". Nobody here measured it.
**CD10. Romance.** Gaider: player-sexuality costs agency, and you must pick. Players on the other side are "totally justified" by his own words. Larian shipped it to great success and praise; PC Gamer's Brown says it strains belief.
**CD11. Violence.** Williams: must carry meaning, or do less. Alexander: claimed meaning is where it fails; silly is fine. Detroit's writer: needed for stakes. A reviewer: Spec Ops unfairly blames players.
**CD12. McCloud and Cohn.** Panel transitions as the unit, or sequence grammar. McCloud is the teaching standard; Cohn has the lab work.
**CD13. Sensitivity reading.** Publishers call it inclusive; Shriver, Clanchy and Horowitz object. Cases are mostly retro edits. Marz and Simone differ on whether fridging is gender or role.
**CD14. AI quality.** Experts prefer human text to prompted AI; general readers don't (79). Homogeneity studies agree with each other, but at least one paper disputes them (83).
**CD15. Testing.** Klavins: interviews over numbers. Barlow: numbers and players. Valve: retelling.

# Advice that's repeated but has weak support

- **CW1. "Stories have three acts" as a historical fact.** History is thin (2); counts vary (3); the only timing study has one coder who knew the targets (4).
- **CW2. Page-number beats.** Bordwell printed a reader's correction to the claim that scripts are discarded for missing them (2).
