# teaching-design: notes

Read on demand; holds the evidence behind SKILL.md's rules: effect sizes, samples and dates. Each tag's full entry and limits
are in `EVIDENCE-LEARNING.md` (L) and `EVIDENCE-CODE-GAMES-AI.md` (G); P tags in `../psychology-review/EVIDENCE.md`.

## How far to trust this

- Written from a literature review on 2026-11-06 (D22). About 300 sources were confirmed to exist by script; most were read only
  as abstracts.

## What counts as learned

- Unguarded AI: in a trial of about 1,000 high school maths students, GPT-4 raised practice 48% and cut the unaided exam 17%; one
  small trial agreed, two found no difference [G-C1, G-C13].
- Physics crossover (149 students): active classes felt 0.56 SD less effective and tested 0.46 SD higher [L-C16, L-C18, G-C7].
- One maths game (10K and 70K subjects): the most engaging conditions produced the slowest learning; correlational data elsewhere
  runs the other way [G-B7].
- Retrieval practice ties or loses on an immediate test and wins at days; one spacing study found nothing at one week and a large
  effect at four [L-C1, L-C3].

## Practice

- Retrieval: classroom meta-analysis g = 0.499, 222 studies [L-C1].
- Expanding vs equal gaps: g = 0.034, not significant [L-C4].
- Seven practice chances comes from a preprint of a model whose regularity is in live dispute; one 1992 paper finds a moderate
  effect of overlearning, and higher thresholds predicted transfer better in one system [L-C5].
- Interleaving: for words, blocking won [L-C7].
- Productive failure: review of 53 studies, g = 0.36, with instruction first favoured for Grades 2-5 and domain-general skills. One
  search found no independent check of its 0.87 bias-adjusted figure, so plan on 0.36 (the evidence author's judgement, not the
  paper's) [L-C14].

## Guidance

- Low prior knowledge: better with high assistance, d = 0.505. High prior knowledge: better with low assistance, d = -0.428 [L-C8].
- Self-paced mastery programmes "often reduce the completion rates in college classes" [L-C6, G-B9].
- Same programming course, not randomised: 45% retained on a schedule, 13% self-paced, "mostly because of different starts" [G-A12].
- Planning prompts: across about 250,000 MOOC students they didn't raise completion, though some raised early engagement. Sustained
  self-regulation training: g = .38, university students [L-C19].

## Feedback and hints

- Computer-based feedback: elaborated 0.49, correct answer 0.32, right/wrong 0.05. Over a third of feedback interventions lowered
  performance [L-C12].
- Four in-game hint designs all lowered performance slightly (50,000 children, minutes of play, not on-demand tutoring), and concrete
  hints were used more and won more levels. Elsewhere, help beat no help in algebra and statistics [G-C10].

## Confidence and motivation

- Within a person, past performance predicted later self-efficacy (ρ = .40) more than self-efficacy predicted performance (.23, or
  .06 with the trend controlled) [P6].
- Wise feedback: among White students, effects on motivation and revision were small or absent [P19].

## AI tutors

- Teaching prompt: preregistered trial of 1,059 students, no effect on any confirmatory measure (read only through a summary). The
  main positive trial (N = 194) ran a whole structured lesson with expert solutions [G-C2].
- Withholding: one preprint system, with no learners measured, reports that a model pressed by a frustrated student doesn't
  withhold reliably on a prompt alone [G-C12].
- Syntax vs plan: inference from n = 12 [G-C5].
- Asking for the next step improved how well learners judged their own ability, and improved unaided use [G-C4].
- Paste raised solution requests; 42% of them came with no attempt [G-C3].
- LLM help (N = 274) failed quality checks on 32% of problems, down to near 0% for algebra and 13% for statistics after a
  self-consistency step, and its gains matched human help. In another study (n = 106, C), hand-written explanations still beat
  GPT-4 [G-C11].
- Offering GPT-4 in a free cohort coding course (5,831 students) cut exam participation 4.3 points, and 14.2% used it. The
  announcement email may be the cause, and adopters' estimated exam gain was +6.8 points [G-C6].
- Pooled AI meta-analyses: one audit, a preprint, finds adjusted SMD 0.196, interval reaching 0 [G-C8]. The newest model tested was
  GPT-4o ("What I looked for" in the G file).

## Story, rewards and games

- Removing a detective theme: d = -0.16, not significant [G-B3].
- Irrelevant detail: persistent g = 0.43 vs 0.12 for transient [L-C23, G-B5].
- Gamification: cognitive g = .49 pooled; game fiction didn't add to the cognitive effect [G-B1].
- Side objectives that reinforce the main goal were consistently useful [G-B10].
- Rewards: completion rewards d = -0.36 on free-choice motivation, positive feedback d = 0.33, with critics [L-C21, G-B2].
- Utility-value trials were mostly in college [P9].

## Voice, text and motion

- Text leads, voice added: g = -0.04. Voice leads, text added: g = 0.29 on average [L-C24].
- Key terms vs full text: g = .526, k = 7, vs .158, k = 37, a mixed moderator from one thesis [L-C24].
- On-screen text beat narrating agents in one meta-analysis; modality effects shrink from d = .72 to .38 to .20 after bias
  correction, reversing in one learner-paced study [G-B5, L-C23].
- Signalling g = 0.43, contiguity g = 0.74, both reported through one overview [L-C23].
- Meaningful animation g = 0.40, decorative g = -0.05 [L-C26].

## Don't design around

- Growth-mindset lessons: d = 0.05, not significant after bias correction [L-C22].
- Learner control: g = 0.05 overall [L-C20].
