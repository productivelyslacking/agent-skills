# production-stages: notes

Read on demand; holds the evidence behind SKILL.md's rules: the cases, numbers and dates. Each tag's full entry and limits
are in `EVIDENCE-PRODUCTION.md`.

## How far to trust this

- Written on 2026-11-06 from a review, not from use (D25). Its first real test is a story-led game's first owner playthrough
  and the human tests around it.
- Most sources were reached second-hand, Pixar's numbers and stories included.

## The biggest alarm

- *The Thief and the Cobbler*: about $28M, more animation than an 87-minute feature needed, key story sequences missing, taken
  over, about 18 minutes of finished animation cut. The source leads with budget, schedule and a rival film, and says its director
  may have meant to trim. One case, through Wikipedia [F-C36].
- One studio's cancelled project with polished cutscenes first failed on its business model; self-reported [G-C3].
- Lightyear had about seven reviews and lost an estimated $106M, though analysts blamed other things [F-C6].
- Cats reportedly spent six months on a two-minute trailer, leaving about four for the other 110 minutes [F-C49, F-C9].

## Stages: what each must prove

- Vertical slice: One designer calls slices wasteful and done for publishers; another warns of "demo hell"; a vendor describes slices
  with some voice and rough or borrowed effects [G-C6, G-C1, G-C8].

## Tests attach to commitments

- Edith Finch cut stories before their characters were written; its director judges a stricter schedule would have cut its
  hardest scenes [G-C37, G-C45].
- Pixar's reel was remade about seven times on one film (one editor, second-hand); Brave's director rebuilt four times in 18
  months (a rescue, after the first director was replaced); a 12-18 week cycle is cited [F-C1, F-C2, F-C4].

## Keep text apart from picture and voice

- One writer rewrote a whole scene in half a day, 10 weeks before ship; "no cost" is his phrase, he concedes fidelity, and it's a tiny
  team with its own engine. Max Payne chose panels partly because they reorganise easily [G-C33, F-C37].
- Voice practice: a vendor and one interview. As far as read, US animation records voice first, anime after [G-C8, G-C39, F-C1, F-C8].

## Cheap tests first

- Rough vs finished: two experiments found usability problems survive low fidelity; a 60-person phone study found looks shift
  emotion and ratings; one director's team didn't feel a story's weight until assets came together. No controlled games study,
  and no account of how film researchers correct for it [G-C11, G-C12, G-C7, G-C9, F-C21].

## A separate look test

- Sonic's design failed in a public trailer; the redesign reportedly cost about $5M (about five months, reportedly no overtime)
  [F-C47].
- Most coded moves in 95 online motion comics were simple [F-C38].

## Lock the spine, finish scenes on evidence

- Supergiant set main beats early and explored details with players, in a replayable roguelike. One showrunner had a direction and no
  fixed roadmap. One director's board changed dialogue, not plot. Against: Edith Finch's story arrived late, and one director starts without an
  ending and calls it dangerous [G-C38, F-C30, DR-3, G-C37, F-C11].
- Locks: mixers say true picture lock has been eroding for decades; half of 24 postmortems reported very late changes [F-C12, G-C5].

## The change list

- Bourne's new ending reportedly cost $200,000 and tested 10 points higher, with no comparison [F-C47, F-C52]. Post departments
  work from change lists [G-C14].
- One self-report survey (273 usable responses; retrospective, volunteers, cancelled projects excluded, ambition unmeasured):
  frequent change to core design correlated -0.44 with outcome, leaders being able to explain a change +0.45, the final game
  resembling the initial vision +0.57 [G-C26].
- Reported reconform costs run from about a day to rebuilding from scratch. In 171 software projects later fixes took no
  consistent extra effort: defects, not creative rework, and it doesn't show late change is cheap [F-C13, G-C15].

## Testers: a budget

- Six allows a no-show; twelve per user type, a hundred for numbers: one researcher's pragmatic figures. Random sets of five found
  55-99% of usability problems [G-C21, G-C22].
- Network executives say tests diagnose better than they forecast; one researcher reports an unpublished study where strong pilots
  did over twice as well as moderate ones; no study of screening scores against results was found [F-C26, F-C27, F-C17, F-C18,
  F-C28, DR-50].
